The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

Mr. Mensah held up an apple and let it fall into his other hand. "Here is a question for scientists," he said. "The Earth pulls the apple down. Does the apple pull the Earth up?" Half of the class said yes, and half said no. "Let's build an with evidence," he said, "and our evidence comes from a man who weighed the world."

He drew on the board. "In 1797 and 1798, in London, a very shy scientist named Henry Cavendish used a that his friend John Michell had built before he died. It was a wooden rod about six feet long, hanging from a thin wire, with a small lead ball at each end. Next to them, Cavendish placed two big lead balls that weighed about 350 pounds each."

When the big balls were placed near the small ones, the rod turned a tiny bit: the big balls pulled the small ones toward them. always ; it never pushes objects away. "So any two objects pull on each other?" asked Elías. "Even you and me," said Mr. Mensah.

"Then why don't I feel Daniel pulling me?" asked Selena. "Because the pull depends on mass," said Mr. Mensah. "The more mass two objects have, the stronger the gravity between them. Daniel has very little mass compared with the Earth, so his pull is far too small to feel."

The pull between the lead balls was so weak that even the warmth of a person's body could stir the air and ruin the measurement. So Cavendish closed the instrument inside a room and never went in. He moved the balls with ropes from outside and read the tiny movements through a telescope pointed through the wall.

From the size of that tiny pull, Cavendish calculated how much mass the Earth must have. He found that the Earth's is about five and a half times that of water, much greater than the density of the rocks on its surface. So the inside of the Earth must be made of something heavier, like the iron of its . His result was within about one percent of the value scientists use today.

"Now, back to the apple," said Mr. Mensah. "The apple does pull the Earth, with a force exactly as big as the Earth's pull on the apple. But the Earth has so much mass that its motion barely changes, while the apple falls." Athena smiled. "Newton's third law again."

Israel looked at the drawing of the closed room. "He weighed the whole world without leaving his house," he said. Sophia wrote in her notebook: Every object pulls every other object. Mass decides how much.

Glossary

gravity
The force of attraction between any two objects that have mass.
gravedad: La fuerza de atracción entre dos objetos cualesquiera que tienen masa.
attract
To pull toward. Gravity always attracts; it never pushes objects apart.
atraer: Tirar hacia sí. La gravedad siempre atrae; nunca separa los objetos.
torsion balance
An instrument with a rod hanging from a thin wire. A very small force makes the wire twist, and the twist can be measured.
balanza de torsión: Un instrumento con una varilla que cuelga de un alambre fino. Una fuerza muy pequeña hace que el alambre se tuerza, y la torsión se puede medir.
density
How much mass is packed into a certain volume.
densidad: Cuánta masa hay en un volumen determinado.
core
The center of the Earth, made mostly of iron and nickel.
núcleo: El centro de la Tierra, hecho sobre todo de hierro y níquel.
argument
In science, a claim supported by evidence and reasoning.
argumento: En ciencias, una afirmación apoyada con evidencia y razonamiento.

Questions

Elige la respuesta correcta. Si te equivocas, el cuento te muestra dónde está la respuesta.

  1. AQuestion A. What happened when the big lead balls were placed near the small ones?

    ¿Qué pasó cuando se colocaron las bolas grandes de plomo cerca de las pequeñas?

    The big balls attracted the small ones, and the rod turned a tiny bit. Gravity always attracts.

  2. BQuestion B. Why can't Selena feel Daniel's gravity?

    ¿Por qué Selena no siente la gravedad de Daniel?

    The pull depends on mass. Daniel's mass is tiny compared with the Earth's, so his pull is too small to feel.

  3. CQuestion C. Why did Cavendish never go into the room with the instrument?

    ¿Por qué Cavendish nunca entraba en el cuarto del instrumento?

    The pull was so weak that air moved by body heat could ruin it, so he read it with a telescope from outside.

  4. DQuestion D. What did Cavendish's result show about the inside of the Earth?

    ¿Qué mostró el resultado de Cavendish sobre el interior de la Tierra?

    The Earth is about five and a half times as dense as water, denser than surface rocks, so its inside must be heavier, like an iron core.

  5. EQuestion E. Does the apple pull the Earth?

    ¿La manzana tira de la Tierra?

    The apple pulls the Earth with the same force, but the Earth has so much mass that its motion barely changes.

Check your thinking

Evidence 1: in Cavendish's experiment, the big lead balls pulled the small ones toward them; the rod never turned the other way, so gravity attracts. Evidence 2: we feel the pull of the enormous Earth, but not the pull of a classmate. Reasoning: the Earth has much more mass than a person, so its gravity is much stronger.

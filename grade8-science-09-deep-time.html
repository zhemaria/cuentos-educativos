Mr. Mensah showed a picture of a rocky point by the sea in Scotland. At the bottom, gray rock layers stood straight up, like books on a shelf. On top of them, red layers lay almost flat. "In June 1788," he said, "a scientist named James Hutton took a boat trip along this coast with two friends, John Playfair and James Hall. When they reached this place, Siccar Point, Hutton knew he had found his proof."

"Proof of what?" asked Elías. ", sand and mud, settles in flat layers at the bottom of the sea," said Mr. Mensah. "So the gray layers were once flat, too. Something must have pushed them up until they stood on end. That takes an enormous amount of time."

"Then the tops of the gray layers were worn down by , and new red layers of sediment slowly piled up on top of them," said Mr. Mensah. "A place where rocks of very different ages meet like this is called an ." He drew the steps on the board: flat layers, pushed up, worn down, new layers on top.

"Hutton's friend Playfair wrote that looking at these rocks, the mind seemed to grow dizzy," said Mr. Mensah, "because they were looking so far back into the abyss of time." "In Hutton's time, many people believed the Earth was only a few thousand years old. The rocks told him it had to be much, much older."

"How old is it really?" asked Selena. "About 4.6 billion years," said Mr. Mensah. "That number is too big to imagine, so let's unroll it." The class stretched a long strip of paper down the hallway, 46 meters long. Each meter stood for 100 million years.

"In undisturbed , the oldest layers are at the bottom and the youngest are at the top," said Mr. Mensah, "so scientists read the layers, and the fossils in them, like the pages of a book. That's how they built the , which divides Earth's history into eras and periods."

They marked the strip. The first simple life appeared in the first quarter of the strip. The dinosaurs appeared less than three meters from the end and disappeared 66 centimeters from the end. Salma looked for humans. "We're the last few millimeters," she said. "The very end of the roll."

Mr. Mensah smiled. "Hutton wrote that he found no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end." Daniel looked down the long hallway at the paper. Sophia wrote in her notebook: Rocks keep a calendar. You just have to learn to read it from the bottom up. That is .

Glossary

rock layers (strata)
Layers of rock, one on top of another. In undisturbed layers, the oldest are at the bottom.
capas de roca (estratos): Capas de roca, una encima de otra. Si no se han movido, las más antiguas están abajo.
sediment
Bits of sand, mud and rock that settle in layers and can harden into rock.
sedimento: Pedacitos de arena, lodo y roca que se depositan en capas y pueden endurecerse hasta formar roca.
erosion
The slow wearing away of rock and soil by water, wind and ice.
erosión: El desgaste lento de las rocas y del suelo por el agua, el viento y el hielo.
unconformity
A place where two sets of rock layers of very different ages meet, showing that a long time passed between them.
discordancia: Un lugar donde se juntan dos grupos de capas de roca de edades muy distintas, lo que muestra que pasó mucho tiempo entre ellos.
geologic time scale
A calendar that divides Earth's 4.6-billion-year history into eras and periods, based on rock layers and fossils.
escala de tiempo geológico: Un calendario que divide los 4600 millones de años de historia de la Tierra en eras y períodos, según las capas de roca y los fósiles.
deep time
The idea that Earth's history is extremely long, measured in millions and billions of years.
tiempo profundo: La idea de que la historia de la Tierra es larguísima y se mide en millones y miles de millones de años.

Questions

  1. AQuestion A. How did Hutton know that the gray layers had been pushed up?

    ¿Cómo supo Hutton que las capas grises habían sido levantadas?

    Sediment settles flat at the bottom of the sea, so layers that stand on end must have been pushed up over a long time.

  2. BQuestion B. What is an unconformity?

    ¿Qué es una discordancia?

    At an unconformity, old worn-down layers lie under much younger ones, showing that a long time passed between them.

  3. CQuestion C. What did the rocks tell Hutton about the age of the Earth?

    ¿Qué le dijeron las rocas a Hutton sobre la edad de la Tierra?

    Many people thought the Earth was only a few thousand years old, but the rocks showed it had to be much, much older.

  4. DQuestion D. In undisturbed rock layers, where are the oldest layers?

    En capas de roca que no se han movido, ¿dónde están las más antiguas?

    Layers pile up over time, so the oldest are at the bottom and the youngest at the top.

  5. EQuestion E. On the 46-meter strip, where were humans?

    En la tira de 46 metros, ¿dónde estaban los seres humanos?

    Humans appear only in the last few millimeters: a tiny part of Earth's history.

Construct an explanation

Construye una explicación. Un acantilado tiene tres capas de roca: A abajo, con fósiles de peces; B en medio, con fósiles de helechos; y C arriba, sin fósiles. ¿Qué capa es la más antigua? Usa evidencia y la idea de la escala de tiempo geológico para explicar cómo lo sabes.

Answer in your notebook or talk it over with a classmate. Use evidence from the story.

Responde en tu cuaderno o coméntalo con un compañero. Usa evidencia del cuento.

Check your thinking

Layer A is the oldest, because in undisturbed layers the bottom layer formed first and the others piled up on top. B is younger than A, and C is the youngest. The fossils tell a story too: when layer A formed, this place was probably under water, because it has fish fossils; later, when B formed, ferns grew here. Scientists compare layers and fossils from many places to build the geologic time scale.

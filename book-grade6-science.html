Chapter 1: The invisible building blocks

Atoms join to make molecules and crystals

The story

On the first day of sixth grade science, Ms. Rivera dumped a giant box of plastic building bricks onto the lab table. "Middle school science starts with a big question," she said. "What is everything made of?"

Daniel picked up a red brick. "Bricks?" he joked. "Close," said Ms. Rivera. "Everything around you, your desk, the air and even you, is made of tiny pieces called . They are so small that a million of them lined up would barely cross the period at the end of a sentence."

She held up bricks of three colors. "Scientists know of 118 kinds of atoms, and each kind is called an . Let's say that white is hydrogen, red is oxygen and black is carbon."

Sophia snapped two white bricks onto one red brick. "It looks like a little face with two ears," she said. Ms. Rivera laughed. "Actually, you just built a model of a of water: two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom. That's why scientists write water as H₂O."

Israel put one black brick between two red ones. "What's mine?" "That's carbon dioxide," said Ms. Rivera, "a gas you breathe out: one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms." "Water and carbon dioxide are both , because each one is made of different kinds of atoms joined together." Then Elías snapped two red bricks together. "Mine is just oxygen and oxygen." "That's the oxygen you breathe in," she said. "It's a molecule, but not a compound, because both atoms are the same element."

Salma had been quietly stacking bricks into a long, flat pattern. "I made a floor," she said. Ms. Rivera picked up a salt shaker. "Salt is a compound too, but it doesn't form small separate molecules. Its atoms connect in a pattern that repeats again and again in every direction, making a ." She shook a few grains onto a black card, and Salma looked at them with a magnifying glass. Every grain was a tiny cube.

Athena frowned. "But atoms aren't really red and white, right?" "Great question," said Ms. Rivera. "Atoms have no colors. Our bricks are a . A model helps us explain something too small to see, but no model is perfect." Athena wrote in her notebook: Real atoms are not bricks, but bricks help us think.

At the end of class, Daniel held up the biggest structure on the table. "What is it?" asked Selena. "A molecule of sugar," he said proudly. Ms. Rivera counted six black bricks, twelve white bricks and six red bricks. "Glucose," she said. "Correct!" Daniel bowed, and two bricks fell off his sugar.

Chapter 2: The race of the melting ice pops

Thermal energy changes particle motion and state

Grade 6 · Science · MS-PS1-4

The story

On the hottest day of July, Selena and Elías bought mango ice pops at the paletería on 18th Street. "Race you," said Elías. "The one who finishes first without a drop on the sidewalk wins." Sophia, who was in a summer science camp, sat on the bench with a thermometer and a cup of ice water. "I'll be the judge," she said.

The ice pops started dripping almost right away. "Why does it melt so fast?" asked Selena, licking her wrist. "Because the hot air is giving it ," said Sophia. "Inside the ice pop, the were packed together and only shaking in place. When they gain energy, they move faster and start sliding past each other."

"So the solid turns into a liquid," said Elías, catching a drip with his tongue. "Exactly. That's called . The ice pop doesn't disappear. It just changes its , from solid to liquid."

Sophia pointed at her cup. The ice cubes were floating in water, and the thermometer said 0 degrees Celsius. "Here's the weird part," she said. "While ice is melting, the stays the same. The energy goes into breaking the ice apart, not into making it warmer. Only when all the ice has melted does the water start to warm up."

Selena's grandmother came out of the shop carrying a pot of water for the elotes. She set it on a little camping stove, and soon bubbles rose from the bottom. "Now the water is so hot that bubbles of gas form all through the liquid, not just at the top," said Sophia. "That's . Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level."

"Can it go the other way?" asked Elías. Sophia nodded and held the cold cup against the steam. Tiny drops of water appeared on the outside of the cup. "When particles lose thermal energy, they slow down and come closer together. A gas can turn back into a liquid, and a liquid can freeze into a solid."

"So heat speeds the particles up, and cold slows them down," said Selena. "Cold just means less thermal energy," said Sophia. "Remove enough energy, and the particles almost stop wiggling."

By then, Elías had mango all the way to his elbows, and Selena had a sticky stick and a clean sidewalk. "Who wins?" they asked. Sophia checked the sidewalk carefully. "Nobody," she said. "Thermal energy wins."

Chemical reactions make new substances

Grade 6 · Science · MS-PS1-2

The story

Israel's grandfather pulled his old bicycle out of the garage. Twenty years ago it had been shiny and silver. Now it was covered in an orange, crusty coat. "It turned orange all by itself," said Abuelo. "Like magic."

Salma, who had come over for breakfast, shook her head. "It's not magic. It's , and it's chemistry." She took two nails from her backpack, one new and shiny, one old and orange. "Ms. Rivera gave us these for homework. We have to compare their ."

They made a table of . The new nail was gray, smooth and hard, and a magnet grabbed it right away. The rusty part was orange, rough and crumbly, and the magnet barely pulled it. "Different color, different texture, different magnetism," said Salma. "That means the rust is a new substance."

"Where did it come from?" asked Israel. "The iron in the bike reacted with oxygen from the air and with water from the rain," said Salma. "Their atoms rearranged and made iron oxide, which we call rust. That's a ."

Abuelo scratched his head. "But when I bent the handlebar years ago, it was still iron." "Right," said Salma. "Bending changed the shape, but it was still the same substance. That's a . No new substance was made."

Inside, Israel's mom was making pancakes. Israel watched the batter bubble on the hot pan. "Is this a chemical reaction too?" "Yes!" said Salma. "The baking powder reacts and makes that puff the pancake up. And the pancake changes color and smell. You can't turn a pancake back into batter."

"So how do I know when a reaction happened?" asked Israel, with his mouth full. "Compare the properties before and after," said Salma. "New colors, new smells, gas bubbles or a change in temperature are clues. But the real test is that the new stuff has different properties from what you started with."

After breakfast, Abuelo found a can of paint. "If air and water make rust," he said, "then paint should keep them away from the iron." Israel grinned. "Abuelo, you're a chemist." "No," said Abuelo, picking up a brush. "I'm a man who wants his bike back."

Chapter 4: The great sled race

Forces and mass change motion

Grade 6 · Science · MS-PS2-2

A story about how forces and mass change the way things move.

La gran carrera de trineos. Un cuento sobre cómo las fuerzas y la masa cambian la manera en que se mueven las cosas.

Science words

The story

The first big snow of the year turned the hill at Humboldt Park into a sledding race track. "New rule," said Sophia, holding up a stopwatch. "This isn't just a race. It's an investigation for Ms. Rivera. We're testing what changes how a sled moves."

First, Daniel pulled an empty sled across the flat snow at the bottom of the hill. It slid easily. Then Athena sat on it, and he pulled with the same strength. The sled crawled. "With Athena on it, the sled has more ," said Sophia, writing it down. "The same pull makes a sled with more mass speed up more slowly."

"My turn!" said Israel. He grabbed one end of the rope on the second sled, and Elías grabbed the other end. They pulled as hard as they could in opposite directions. The sled didn't move at all. "Two equal in opposite directions," said Sophia. "They're , so they cancel out, and the motion doesn't change."

Then Daniel ran over and helped Israel pull. The sled shot toward them, and Elías landed face first in the snow. "Now the forces are ," said Sophia. "Two people pulling one way is more than one person pulling the other way, so the sled moves toward the bigger force."

At the top of the hill, they raced the sleds down. The sled on the icy path zoomed. The sled on the bumpy, grassy path slowed down halfway. "The grass and bumps make more ," said Athena. "Friction is a force too. It pushes against the motion and slows the sled down."

Elías, brushing snow off his face, had an idea. "Let's see who's fastest: me alone on one sled, or Daniel and Athena together on the other, on different paths." Sophia shook her head. "That's not a fair test. If we change the mass and the path at the same time, we won't know which one made the difference. We have to change only one at a time."

So they did it the scientist's way. Same path, same push from the top, one rider, then two riders. They ran each test three times, and Sophia wrote down every time on the stopwatch. The light sled always started faster with the same push.

On the walk home, Daniel looked at the notebook full of numbers. "So the whole investigation proves one thing," he said. "Forces and mass decide how things move." "And one more thing," said Elías, still shaking snow out of his hood. "Never be on the side with fewer people."

Glossary

force
A push or a pull. Forces can start, stop or change the motion of an object.
fuerza: Un empujón o un jalón. Las fuerzas pueden poner en movimiento, detener o cambiar el movimiento de un objeto.
mass
The amount of matter in an object. More mass makes an object harder to speed up or slow down.
masa: La cantidad de materia que tiene un objeto. Con más masa, cuesta más acelerarlo o frenarlo.
balanced forces
Forces that are equal in size and pull or push in opposite directions, so the motion does not change.
fuerzas equilibradas: Fuerzas del mismo tamaño que empujan o jalan en direcciones opuestas, así que el movimiento no cambia.
unbalanced forces
Forces that do not cancel out, so they change the speed or direction of an object.
fuerzas no equilibradas: Fuerzas que no se anulan, así que cambian la rapidez o la dirección de un objeto.
friction
A force that slows things down when two surfaces rub against each other.
fricción: Una fuerza que frena las cosas cuando dos superficies se rozan.
variable
A part of an experiment that can change. In a fair test, you change only one variable at a time.
variable: Una parte de un experimento que puede cambiar. En una prueba justa, se cambia una sola variable a la vez.

Grade 6 · Science · MS-PS3-3

The story

Salma's grandmother made two dozen tamales for the sixth grade potluck. The problem was the schedule. The tamales would come out of the steamer at 11:00, but the potluck didn't start until 1:00. "Cold tamales are a tragedy," said Salma.

Ms. Rivera turned the problem into a design challenge. "Your : the tamales must still be warm, above 60 degrees Celsius, after two hours. Your : you can only use materials from this table, and the container must fit in a backpack."

On the table were a metal tray, foam cups, a wool scarf, aluminum foil, newspaper, towels and a small foam cooler. "First, why do warm things get cold?" asked Ms. Rivera. "Thermal energy always moves from warmer things to colder things," said Daniel. "The hot tamales give their energy to the cooler air and to anything they touch. That's ."

Athena touched the metal tray and then a foam cup. "The metal feels colder, even though they're both at room temperature." "That's because metal is a good ," said Ms. Rivera. "It pulls thermal energy out of your warm hand quickly by . The foam is an , so it slows the energy down."

So the team built a design. They wrapped the pot in a towel, put it in the foam cooler, and stuffed crumpled newspaper around it. "Crumpled paper traps lots of tiny pockets of air," said Salma. "Trapped air is a great insulator, because it can't move around and carry the energy away."

Then they tested it. They put a thermometer in one tamale inside the cooler and another thermometer in one tamale left on the metal tray. Every half hour, they wrote down both temperatures. After two hours, the tamale on the tray was at 28 degrees. The one in the cooler was still at 61.

"Success!" shouted Daniel. But Ms. Rivera asked one more question. "How could you make it even better?" Athena looked closely at the cooler. "The lid didn't close tightly. Warm air escaped there. Next time we can seal the lid with tape and test again." "That's exactly what engineers do," said Ms. Rivera. "Test, improve, test again."

At one o'clock, Salma opened the cooler, and steam rose into the room. The whole class lined up. Ms. Rivera took the last tamale and closed her eyes. "This," she said, "is the most delicious science experiment I have ever graded."

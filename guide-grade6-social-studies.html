Connecting history and the arts

How to use this guide

Each story has a pause to connect that a child can do alone on a tablet: look at a work of art from the time or read lines from a public domain poem. The activities in this guide are for the whole class or small groups. They are optional.

The arts connections follow Anchor Standard 11 (Connecting) of the Illinois Arts Learning Standards. The stories use only well-established historical facts and do not put invented words in the mouths of real people. When students write from the point of view of a historical person, present it clearly as their own creative writing.

In Grade 6, the Illinois standards for history and civics describe two levels of complexity. Each story's challenge works at the grade level (LC). The optional Go further question asks for more complex reasoning (MdC), for students who are ready for it.

Activities by story

1

The box from 1976

Primary and secondary sources · SS.6-8.H.3.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Class time capsule

VA:Cn11.1.6

Each group brings or draws three objects for a class time capsule and writes a museum label for each one. On the back of each label, the group writes whether the object is a primary or a secondary source and one thing a future historian could NOT learn from it. Seal the labels in a box to open at the end of the year.

Discussion questions

Who made this source, when and why? Why might two people from the same year describe it in different ways?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

In a museum, every artifact has a small label next to it. The label tells visitors what the object is, when it was made and why it matters. Imagine that the cassette tape from the time capsule is now in a museum.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.H.3.LC: classify the kinds of historical sources (primary and secondary) and explain what each one can and cannot tell us about the past. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.H.3.LC; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

2

The river that gave and took

Rivers, irrigation and the first cities · SS.6-8.G.4 · SS.6-8.G.7

Open the story

Group activity: Clay records · A peace panel

VA:Cn11.1.6

Give each group a ball of clay or play dough and a craft stick. Students press simple marks to keep a record of "goods" in the classroom (pencils, books, chairs), as early scribes kept records of grain and animals. Then groups draw a long strip in three rows, like the peace side of the Standard of Ur, showing the goods and jobs of their own neighborhood.

Discussion questions

How did the environment decide where people settled? Which changes to the rivers helped people, and which caused new problems?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the Standard of Ur, a wooden box decorated more than 4,500 years ago in the city of Ur, in Mesopotamia. Look at each row, one at a time.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.G.4 · SS.6-8.G.7: explain how people interact with and change their environment, and how the environment affects where people settle. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.G.4; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

3

The gift of the Nile

Change and continuity along the Nile · SS.6-8.H.1.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Then-and-now diptych

VA:Cn11.1.6

Each student folds a paper in half. On the left, they draw a scene from ancient Egypt from the story (the flood, a shaduf, a boat on the Nile). On the right, they draw the same place or activity today. The class hangs the drawings and sorts them into "mostly change" and "mostly continuity."

Discussion questions

What changed along the Nile, and what stayed the same? Why do some things last for thousands of years?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at this small blue hippopotamus. It was made in Egypt nearly 4,000 years ago and placed in a tomb. Today, museum visitors call it William.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.H.1.LC: analyze change and continuity over time, using the example of how people have lived along the Nile River from ancient Egypt to today. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.H.1.LC; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

4

Everybody votes?

Democracy in Athens and today · SS.6-8.CV.4.LC · SS.6-8.H.2.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Assembly vote · Design a class coin

VA:Cn11.1.6

Hold a direct vote on a real class choice. Then repeat it, but this time only students born in the first half of the year may vote. Discuss how the excluded students felt, and connect it to who could vote in Athens. Finally, groups design a class coin with one symbol on each side, like the owl and Athena, and explain what the symbols say about the class.

Discussion questions

How was Athens a democracy, and how was it not? How have ideas about who can vote changed over time?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at a silver coin made in Athens around 450 years before the Common Era. Athenians used coins like this to buy and sell.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.CV.4.LC · SS.6-8.H.2.LC: describe democratic principles in ancient Athens and today, and explain how ideas about who can take part in government have changed over time. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.CV.4.LC; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

5

Rules on the wall

The Twelve Tables and written laws · SS.6-8.CV.2.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Our twelve tables · Arch challenge

VA:Cn11.1.6

The class writes and votes on twelve short rules, posts them on the wall and signs them. As an extension, groups build an arch and a flat bridge of the same width out of paper or cardboard, then test which one holds more coins, to see why Roman builders used arches in aqueducts like the Pont du Gard.

Discussion questions

Why did the plebeians want the laws written down? Can a written law still be unfair?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the Pont du Gard, a Roman aqueduct bridge in what is now southern France. The Romans built it about 2,000 years ago to carry water to the city of Nîmes.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.CV.2.LC: explain the origins and purposes of written laws and governing documents, from the Twelve Tables of Rome to classroom rules. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.CV.2.LC; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

6

Pass it down the Silk Road

Trade and ideas on the Silk Road · SS.6-8.G.8

Open the story

Group activity: Silk Road trading game

VA:Cn11.1.6

Line up desks as cities, as in the story. Pass a "good" (a scarf or a card) and raise its price at each city, then pass a whispered sentence and compare the first and last versions. Students mark the routes on a map and add one good and one idea that traveled in each direction.

Discussion questions

How did transportation help goods and ideas spread? Why did ideas change as they traveled? What carries ideas around the world today?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at this clay camel, made in China during the Tang dynasty, about 1,300 years ago. Figures like this were placed in tombs.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.G.8: explain how transportation and communication along trade routes like the Silk Road spread goods, ideas and inventions. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.G.8; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

7

The card company

Paper, printing and innovation · SS.6-8.EC.4

Open the story

Group activity: Stamp print shop

VA:Cn11.1.6

Groups carve a simple design into a foam sheet or a potato (with adult help) and print cards with paint or ink. Time how many cards one student makes by hand and how many one student prints in the same three minutes, then make a bar graph to show the change in productivity.

Discussion questions

How did paper and printing change people's work and learning? Who might lose work when a new invention arrives, and how can they adapt?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the Diamond Sutra, a Buddhist book printed in China in the year 868 with woodblocks. It is the oldest printed book we know of that has an exact date.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.EC.4: explain how innovation and entrepreneurship, like the invention of paper and printing, change how people work and live. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.EC.4; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

8

Worth its weight in salt

Gold, salt and the Mali Empire · SS.6-8.G.12

Open the story

Group activity: Illustrated trade map

VA:Cn11.1.6

Groups make an illustrated map of West Africa and the Sahara in the style of the Catalan Atlas: mark where salt and gold came from, draw a camel caravan along the route and add Timbuktu. Then each group adds one picture showing a resource from Illinois and where it is traded today.

Discussion questions

Why did the north and the south need each other? How did controlling trade routes make Mali rich?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at a page of the Catalan Atlas, a map made in 1375 on the island of Majorca, which is now part of Spain, by a mapmaker named Abraham Cresques. Find Mansa Musa on it.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.G.12: explain how the physical environment and resources affected production and trade in the Mali Empire and the world trade network. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.G.12; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

9

A city on a lake

Tenochtitlan and Mexico City · SS.6-8.G.4 · SS.6-8.H.4.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Chinampa model · Cause or correlation?

VA:Cn11.1.6

In a shallow tray of water, groups build a small chinampa with layers of soil and twigs or sticks at the corners, and test whether it holds together. Then groups list pairs of things that happen together (summer and ice cream sales, more swimmers) and sort them into "one causes the other" and "only a correlation."

Discussion questions

How did the Mexica adapt to the lake? Why is Mexico City sinking today? How can we tell a cause from a correlation?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the first picture page of the Codex Mendoza, a book painted by Indigenous artists in Mexico around 1540, about 20 years after the Spanish conquest. It shows the founding of Tenochtitlan.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.G.4 · SS.6-8.H.4.LC: explain how the Mexica changed their environment to build Tenochtitlan, and tell the difference between things that happen together and things that cause each other. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.G.4; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

10

The pumpkin price mystery

Supply, demand and prices · SS.6-8.EC.6

Open the story

Group activity: Classroom market · Find the jobs

VA:Cn11.1.6

Run a short market with paper goods: change the number of goods (supply) or the number of buyers (demand) in each round, and record the prices on a chart. Then project The Harvesters and have pairs list every job they can find in the painting and where the wheat might go next.

Discussion questions

What happens to prices when there is a shortage or a surplus? Is it fair to raise prices when people really need something?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at The Harvesters, painted by Pieter Bruegel the Elder in 1565, in what is now Belgium. It shows a wheat harvest at the end of summer.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.EC.6: explain how supply and demand affect prices in a market. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.EC.6; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

11

The oath

Citizenship, rights and responsibilities · SS.6-8.CV.1.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Rights and responsibilities posters · A class action

VA:Cn11.1.6

Groups make a poster with two columns, rights and responsibilities, using pictures and a few words. Then the class chooses one small action to help the school or neighborhood (a cleanup, a letter to the alderperson, a book drive) and names the right it uses and the responsibility it meets. Important: do not ask students about their own or their families' citizenship or immigration status. Keep the discussion general and focused on the ideas.

Discussion questions

What rights belong to everyone who lives in the United States, and which belong only to citizens? How can young people practice good citizenship before they can vote?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the Statue of Liberty, in New York Harbor. It was a gift from the people of France and was finished in 1886. Read the famous lines from a poem by Emma Lazarus, written in 1883, which are on a bronze plaque inside the statue's pedestal.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.CV.1.LC: describe the roles, rights and responsibilities of citizens in the United States, and explain the different ways a person can become a citizen. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.CV.1.LC; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

12

The museum of us

Review of the whole year · SS.6-8.H · SS.6-8.G · SS.6-8.CV · SS.6-8.EC

Open the story

Group activity: Museum night

VA:Cn11.1.6

Each student or group prepares an exhibit about one big idea of the year, with a central claim, two pieces of evidence, one artifact (a real object, a copy or a drawing) with a museum label and one connection to today. Invite families to visit, as in the story.

Discussion questions

What connections do you see between the ancient world and your life? Whose perspectives did you include in your exhibit?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look back at the art you met this year: the Standard of Ur, the blue hippo from Egypt, the coin from Athens, the Pont du Gard, the Tang camel, the Diamond Sutra, the Catalan Atlas, the Codex Mendoza, The Harvesters and the Statue of Liberty.

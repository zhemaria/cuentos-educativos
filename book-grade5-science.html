Chapter 1: The popcorn mystery

Matter is made of particles too small to see

A story about the tiny particles that make up all matter.

El misterio de las palomitas. Un cuento sobre las partículas diminutas que forman toda la materia.

The story

On the first day of fifth grade science, Ms. Kowalski stood at the back of the room with a popcorn machine. "Before we start," she said, "nobody turn around. Just raise your hand when you notice something."

The machine began to pop. A few seconds later, Selena raised her hand from the very front row. "Popcorn!" she said. "I can smell it all the way up here. But I can't see anything flying through the air."

"Good observation," said Ms. Kowalski. "Here's the mystery: how did the smell get to your nose?" Elías guessed that the popcorn had magic powers. Ms. Kowalski laughed. "Close, but not quite. The answer is that all is made of tiny ."

Tiny particles from the hot popcorn mixed into the air and spread across the room until they reached Selena's nose. "They are much too small to see," the teacher said, "but your nose can detect them."

Then she gave Sophia a glass of water and a spoonful of sugar. Sophia stirred and stirred, and the sugar disappeared. "It's gone," said Israel. "It's not gone," said Ms. Kowalski. "The sugar . It broke into particles too small to see, and they spread out in the water."

"Prove it," said Israel, crossing his arms. Sophia took a tiny sip. "It tastes sweet!" she said. "The sugar is still in the water." "That's ," said Ms. Kowalski. "We can't see the particles, but we can find out that they're there."

Next, Daniel blew into a red balloon until his cheeks looked like apples. The balloon grew bigger and bigger. "But air is nothing," Elías said. "How can nothing make a balloon get so big?"

"Air is not nothing," said the teacher. "Air is a , and a gas is matter too." "Billions of air particles bounce around inside the balloon and push on it from the inside. That pushing is what makes the balloon bigger." Daniel tied the knot and tapped the balloon. It felt tight and full.

Athena was busy drawing in her sketchbook: a balloon full of little dots, and a glass of water with sugar dots spread all around. "This is a ," she said. "It helps me explain things that are too small to see." Ms. Kowalski hung the drawing on the wall. Then she passed out the popcorn, and Israel declared that this was the best mystery he had ever solved with his mouth.

Chapter 2: The bag that puffed up

New substances and weight that stays the same

Grade 5 · Science · 5-PS1-2 · 5-PS1-4

A story about mixing substances and about weight that does not change.

La bolsa que se infló. Un cuento sobre mezclar sustancias y sobre un peso que no cambia.

Science words

The story

Ms. Kowalski set three things on the lab table: a zip-top plastic bag, a small cup of baking soda and a bottle of vinegar. "Today, two are going to meet," she said. "And we're going to weigh what happens."

Daniel put the bag, the cup of baking soda and the vinegar on the digital scale together. Salma wrote down the number in her notebook. Then Daniel poured the baking soda into the bag, and Israel added the vinegar. Daniel zipped the bag shut, fast.

Fssssss! The started to fizz and bubble like a soda that someone had shaken. The bag began to puff up like a pillow. "It's alive!" Israel shouted, jumping back.

"It's not alive," Ms. Kowalski said, "but something new is happening. The baking soda and the vinegar are reacting, and they are making a : a called carbon dioxide." That gas is what's filling up the bag.

Salma touched the bag. "It feels cold," she said. "And the bubbles weren't there before." "Exactly," said the teacher. "When two substances mix and you see bubbles, a change in temperature or a new color, it can be a sign that a new substance formed."

Israel made a prediction. "Gas is light," he said. "So now the bag will weigh less." Daniel put the puffy bag back on the scale, next to the empty cup and the empty bottle. Everyone held their breath.

The scale showed exactly the same number as before. "No way," said Israel. Salma checked her notebook twice. "The same," she said. "Not one gram more, and not one gram less."

"In a sealed bag, nothing can get in and nothing can get out," Ms. Kowalski explained. "The particles just changed partners and formed new substances. So the total is : it stays the same." Then she opened the bag. Pfff! The gas rushed out.

Daniel weighed the open bag again. This time the number was a little smaller, because the gas had escaped into the air of the classroom. Israel wrinkled his nose. "So now we're breathing the experiment?" "A tiny bit," said Ms. Kowalski. "Don't worry. Carbon dioxide is also in the air you breathe out." Israel took a giant breath, just to be sure.

A substance that was not there before, made when two substances react. It has different properties.

Matter that has no shape of its own and spreads out to fill any space.

weight
How heavy something is. We measure it with a scale.

Kept the same. In a sealed bag, the total weight is conserved, even when substances change.

Chapter 3: The case of the white powders

Identifying materials by their properties

Grade 5 · Science · 5-PS1-3

A story about how scientists identify materials by testing their properties.

El caso de los polvos blancos. Un cuento sobre cómo los científicos identifican materiales probando sus propiedades.

Open this story alone

Science words

The story

On Saturday morning, Salma's grandmother washed her four kitchen jars, and all the paper labels slid off in the sink. "Ay, no," she said. "Now I don't know which one is sugar, which is salt, which is flour and which is baking soda." Salma and Selena looked at each other. This was a job for scientists.

"First rule," said Salma, who remembered what Ms. Kowalski always said. "Scientists never taste a mystery powder. It could be dangerous." Her grandmother nodded, impressed. "Then how will you know?" "We'll their ," Selena said.

Salma looked at each powder with a magnifying glass. Two of the powders were made of tiny , with flat sides that sparkled in the light. The other two were soft and fine, like dust. Selena wrote in her notebook: "Jars 1 and 2: crystals. Jars 3 and 4: fine powder."

For the next test, they put one spoonful of each powder into four cups of warm water and stirred the same number of times. The crystals in jars 1 and 2 disappeared. Jar 4 also mixed in. But the water in cup 3 turned cloudy and white, and the powder settled at the bottom. "Jar 3 is not ," said Selena. "It's the flour!"

Then they put a little of each powder in four small dishes and added a few drops of vinegar. Three dishes did nothing. But the powder from jar 4 started to fizz and bubble right away. "It with vinegar," Salma said. "That's the baking soda." Her grandmother clapped.

Two jars were left: sugar and salt. Both were white crystals, and both dissolved in water. "They look like twins," said Selena. "How can we tell them apart without tasting?"

Salma's grandmother had an idea. She put a pinch from jar 1 in one old spoon and a pinch from jar 2 in another, and she held them over the stove for a moment. Only an adult could do this part. The crystals in jar 2 melted and turned into brown, sticky caramel. The crystals in jar 1 did not change at all.

"Jar 2 is sugar, because sugar melts and browns with heat," said Salma. "And jar 1 is salt." Selena wrote new labels in big letters, and they taped them on the jars.

"You didn't guess once," said the grandmother. "You used properties to every powder." Then she made pancakes with the flour, the baking soda and the sugar. "And the salt?" asked Selena. "A pinch," said her grandmother, "for the scientists who earned it."

A solid piece with flat sides and a regular shape, like a grain of salt or sugar.

Able to dissolve in a liquid. Salt is soluble in water; flour is not.

To change when touching another substance, often with bubbles or heat.

Chapter 4: Why the ball always comes down

Gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth

Grade 5 · Science · 5-PS2-1

A story about gravity, the force that pulls everything down.

Por qué el balón siempre baja. Un cuento sobre la gravedad, la fuerza que jala todo hacia abajo.

Science words

The story

After school, Elías, Daniel and Israel went to the basketball court in the park. Elías threw the ball as high as he could. It went up, up, up... and then it came back down, as always, and bounced off Israel's head. "Ow!" said Israel. "Why does everything always fall on me?"

"Not on you," said Daniel, pushing up his glasses. "Down. Everything falls down. It's ." "And what is gravity?" asked Israel, rubbing his head. Daniel had studied this in class with Ms. Kowalski.

"Gravity is a , a pull," Daniel explained. "Earth pulls every object toward it, even if nobody touches it. That's why the ball comes back down." "Even a feather?" asked Elías. "Even a feather. It just falls more slowly because the air gets in its way."

Israel thought for a moment, and then he looked worried. "Wait. If Earth is round, what about the kids on the other side, in Australia? Are they upside down? Why don't they fall off into space?"

Daniel smiled and took a small globe out of his backpack. He had been waiting for this question. All around the globe, he had taped tiny paper people: one in Chicago, one at the North Pole, one in Brazil and one in Australia, on the bottom.

"Earth is a , almost like a big ball," said Daniel. "Gravity pulls everything toward the of Earth. So for every person, 'down' means toward the center of the planet, under their feet." He turned the globe. Every little paper person had their feet on the ground and their head pointing out to space.

"So in Australia, down is under their feet too," said Elías. "Right. Nobody is upside down. Gravity pulls all of them toward the middle of Earth, just like it pulls us." Israel looked relieved. "Good. I'd hate for a kid in Australia to have a ball fall on his head and then keep going into space."

Elías picked up the ball. "Then why do I feel heavy when I jump?" "That's your ," said Daniel. "Your weight is the pull of gravity on you." "So gravity is why I can't dunk," Elías said. "Mostly," Daniel answered. "Being short helps too."

Israel tossed the ball straight up one more time and stepped quickly out of the way. The ball came straight down in the same as always: toward the center of Earth. It bounced right where he had been standing. "Ha!" said Israel. "I finally beat gravity." "No," said Daniel, laughing, "you just finally moved."

Chapter 5: The sunshine taco

The energy in food was once energy from the sun

A story about how the energy in our food comes from the sun.

El taco de luz de sol. Un cuento sobre cómo la energía de nuestros alimentos viene del Sol.

Science words

The story

It was taco day in the cafeteria, and Selena was holding up her chicken taco like a trophy. "This is my favorite lunch," she said. "It gives me so much for recess."

"Do you know where that energy came from?" asked Athena, opening her sketchbook. "From the cafeteria kitchen," said Israel, with his mouth full. "Before that," said Athena. "Ms. Kowalski said that almost all the energy in our started in the sun."

Selena looked at her taco. "There's no sun in my taco. It's a tortilla, chicken and lettuce." Athena drew a big bright sun at the top of the page. "Let's follow the energy backward," she said. "Start with the tortilla."

"Tortillas are made from corn," said Sophia. "And corn is a . The corn plant used to make its own food, and it stored some of that energy in its kernels." Athena drew an arrow from the sun to a tall corn plant.

"And the chicken?" asked Selena. "Chickens can't use sunlight to make food." "No," said Athena, "but the chicken ate corn and seeds. So the chicken got its energy from plants, and the plants got it from the sun." She drew another arrow, from the corn plant to a chicken.

"The lettuce is easy," said Israel. "It's a plant too. It caught sunlight with its leaves." "So every part of my taco is full of energy from the sun," said Selena. "It's a sunshine taco."

Athena drew the last arrow, from the chicken to a girl running across the page. "And now you eat it," she said. "Your body uses that energy to move, like when you run at recess."

"That's not all," said Sophia. "Your body also uses the energy from food to keep its , even when it's cold outside, and to grow." Selena nodded. "And when I scraped my knee last week, my skin healed." "Right. The body uses food to itself, too," said Athena.

Selena looked at her drawing on the sketchbook page: sun, corn, chicken, girl. "So when I run at recess," she said slowly, "I'm really running with sunshine." "Exactly," said Athena. Israel stood up. "Then I'm going to go play soccer with my sunshine," he said, and he grabbed one more taco for the road.

"After five years, the little tree had grown into a big tree," she continued. "It had gained about 164 pounds. Then van Helmont dried and weighed the soil again. It had lost only about two ounces." Elías's eyes went wide. "Two ounces? That's like a small cookie!"

The story

At nine o'clock in the morning, Ms. Kowalski took the class out to the schoolyard with a box of chalk. Salma stood on an X drawn on the ground, and Selena traced her in blue chalk. It was long, very long, and it pointed toward the west, away from the Sun, which was low in the east.

"Does the Sun move?" asked Selena. Back in the classroom, Sophia turned off the lights. She held a flashlight still, like the Sun, and slowly turned a globe. "The Sun doesn't move across the sky," she said. "Earth on its . As it spins, the Sun seems to move from east to west, and the shadows change."

"And it happens every day, the same way," said Salma. "Morning, long shadow. Noon, short shadow. Afternoon, long again." "That's a ," said the teacher. "Patterns help scientists predict what will happen next."

"Why does this matter so much?" asked Israel, holding up a soggy chip bag. "Because clean water is a that everyone needs," said Ms. Kowalski. "Chicago gets its drinking water from Lake Michigan, and fish, birds and plants need clean water too."

Selena found a tiny piece of plastic on the shore. "Plastic doesn't rot like a banana peel," the teacher said. "Decomposers can't break it down. It breaks into smaller and smaller pieces, and that can harm fish and birds that swallow it."

"So what can we do?" asked Sophia. "Communities use science ideas to their resources," said Ms. Kowalski. "Some cities put out more recycling bins. Some ban plastic bags. And many people volunteer to clean rivers and beaches, like we're doing today."

Back at school on Monday, the teacher gave them an engineering challenge. "Design a trash catcher that stops floating trash in a stream." She wrote the on the board: it must catch floating trash, and it must let water and fish pass through. Then she wrote the : you can use only materials from the recycling bin, and you have one week.

Selena, Israel and Sophia built their first : a plastic crate with a piece of window screen tied across it. They tested it in a long tray of water with floating bottle caps. The crate caught some of the caps, but the water pushed the whole thing over.

"It failed," said Israel, disappointed. "No," said Sophia, writing in her notebook. "It taught us something." They tied two heavy rocks to the bottom of the crate and tested it again. This time it stayed in place and caught almost every cap, and the water flowed right through the screen.

On Friday, the class tested every team's design and compared the results. Some caught more trash, some were stronger, and some were easier to empty. "Engineers test, learn and improve," said Ms. Kowalski. "That's how they solve real problems for their communities."

Before going home, Israel looked at the tray full of rescued bottle caps. "We should take this to the river cleanup next year," he said. "And the fish can send us a thank-you note." "Fish can't write," said Selena. "Then they can send us a thank-you bubble," said Israel.

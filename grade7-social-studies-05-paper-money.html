The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

Ms. Novak handed Elías a cloth bag. He almost dropped it. "What's in here, bricks?" "Play coins," she said, "but just as heavy as real iron coins. About a thousand years ago, in the Sichuan region of China, merchants paid with coins like these. To buy a roll of silk, you might need a cart to carry the money."

"So merchants in Sichuan had an idea," said Ms. Novak. "They left their heavy coins with trusted shops and received a paper receipt. Soon people began to buy and sell with the receipts themselves, because paper was so much easier to carry." Sophia held up a light paper note. "Like this?" "Exactly. That was the beginning of ."

"In 1023, the Song government took over printing the notes," Ms. Novak went on. "It was the first government in the world to issue paper money." The notes were printed with complicated patterns, several colors and official seals. "Why so fancy?" asked Daniel. "To stop notes," said Ms. Novak. "If paper can be money, someone will always try to print their own."

"But why does a piece of paper have value?" asked Sophia. "Because people trust the government that issues it," said Ms. Novak. "A paper note is a promise. As long as people believe the promise, they accept the note as ."

Then she turned the classroom into a market. Each student got 10 paper notes, and a granola bar cost 2 notes. Everyone bought one, and the bars were gone. The next day, she gave everyone 50 notes, but she still had the same number of granola bars. Soon students were offering 5, 8, even 10 notes for one bar. "The price went up!" said Elías. "Nothing changed except the money."

"That's what happened in China," said Ms. Novak. "Later governments needed money to pay for wars, so they printed more and more notes. When the of money grows much faster than the goods people can buy, each note is worth less, and prices rise. That's called ." She showed them a chart of prices that went up steeply, then shot up like a rocket.

"What happened in the end?" asked Daniel, looking at his thick stack of notes. "When people stopped trusting the notes, they wanted coins and silver again," said Ms. Novak. "After a long period of terrible inflation, the Ming emperors stopped printing new paper money, and China didn't issue it again for hundreds of years." The for paper money disappeared because nobody trusted it.

At the end of class, Ms. Novak held up a dollar bill. "Today, almost every country uses paper money, and central banks try to control how much money is printed." Elías handed back the heavy bag. "I'll take the paper," he said, "as long as they don't print too much of it."

What you will see: A large gray note made of mulberry bark paper, much bigger than a dollar bill. A border of dragons surrounds the edge, Chinese writing runs across the top, and in the middle there is a picture of strings of coins. Red seals were stamped on it.

Lo que verás: Un billete grande y gris hecho de papel de corteza de morera, mucho más grande que un billete de dólar. Un borde de dragones rodea el contorno, hay escritura china en la parte de arriba y en el centro un dibujo de sartas de monedas. Tenía sellos rojos estampados.

Think about it, then answer in your notebook or talk it over with someone at home.

Check your thinking

The picture shows how many coins the note was worth, so even people who could not read could understand its value. The dragons were a symbol of the emperor, the red seals showed that the government issued it, and the complicated patterns were hard to copy. Today's bills use the same ideas: portraits, seals and fine patterns.

Glossary

currency
The money used in a country, like coins and paper bills.
moneda: El dinero que se usa en un país, como las monedas y los billetes.
paper money
Printed notes that stand for an amount of money. People accept them because they trust the government that issues them.
papel moneda: Billetes impresos que representan una cantidad de dinero. La gente los acepta porque confía en el gobierno que los emite.
supply
How much of something is available.
oferta: Cuánto hay disponible de algo.
demand
How much of something people want and are able to buy.
demanda: Cuánto quiere y puede comprar la gente de algo.
inflation
A rise in prices over time, which means the same money buys less.
inflación: Una subida de los precios con el tiempo, lo que significa que el mismo dinero compra menos.
counterfeit
A fake copy of money or of something valuable, made to trick people.
falsificación: Una copia falsa del dinero o de algo valioso, hecha para engañar.

Check your thinking

Options: raise taxes (it pays for the war without inflation, but people may be angry); borrow money (it helps now, but it must be paid back); print a little more money (it is fast, but too much causes inflation). A strong answer chooses one option and names both its benefits and its costs.

The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

Ms. Novak wrote two words on the board: algebra and algorithm. "Both of these words," she said, "came from one city and one man, more than 1,200 years ago." Daniel groaned. "Algebra has a history? I thought it was just a punishment."

Ms. Novak laughed and pointed to Baghdad on the map. "In the early 800s, Baghdad was the capital of the Abbasid Empire and one of the largest cities in the world. The who ruled there supported a famous center of learning that people called the House of Wisdom."

"What did they do there?" asked Athena. " of many backgrounds collected books from Greece, Persia and India and worked on their into Arabic," said Ms. Novak. "Ideas that were written in many languages could now be read, compared and improved in one language."

Salma held up a sheet of paper. "Did they write on this?" "Yes, and that's part of the story," said Ms. Novak. "Papermaking came from China. By the late 700s, there were paper mills in Baghdad. Paper was much cheaper than parchment, so books could be copied faster and sent farther along the trade routes."

Then she told them about one scholar, Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi. Around the year 820, he wrote a book about solving problems with unknown numbers, the kind merchants and judges needed for trade and for dividing land. "One step in his method was called al-jabr, which means restoring or completing," said Ms. Novak. "That word became . And when his books were translated into Latin, his name became Algoritmi, which gave us the word ."

Daniel looked at the equation on his whiteboard, x + 5 = 12. "So when I subtract 5 from both sides, I'm doing something from Baghdad?" "You're doing something people have done for more than a thousand years," said Ms. Novak. "Al-Khwarizmi also wrote about the numbers from India, with nine digits and a zero, the numbers you use every day."

"So the ideas went from India and Greece to Baghdad, and then from Baghdad to Europe," said Athena, tracing the arrows on the map. "Exactly. Historians call that ," said Ms. Novak. "Ideas spread along trade routes, carried by translators, merchants, paper and books. No single culture invented math by itself."

Before the bell, Ms. Novak asked them to find one word they used that had traveled from another language. Salma wrote "algebra." Daniel wrote "zero." Athena wrote "sugar," which, she found, had traveled through Arabic too. "The whole room is full of travelers," said Ms. Novak.

Check your thinking

The books lie flat, stacked in square spaces, instead of standing in rows with their spines out. The painting shows that libraries and reading together were important in Baghdad in the 1200s. But the painter never saw the House of Wisdom, so the painting is not a photograph of it: it is a primary source about his own time, and only a clue about earlier times.

Glossary

scholar
A person who studies a subject deeply and shares what they learn.
sabio, erudito: Una persona que estudia un tema a fondo y comparte lo que aprende.
translation
Changing a text from one language into another so more people can read it.
traducción: Pasar un texto de un idioma a otro para que más gente pueda leerlo.
caliph
The title of a ruler of a large Muslim empire, like the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad.
califa: El título del gobernante de un gran imperio musulmán, como los califas abasíes de Bagdad.
algebra
The part of math that uses letters and symbols to find unknown numbers. The word comes from the Arabic al-jabr.
álgebra: La parte de las matemáticas que usa letras y símbolos para hallar números desconocidos. La palabra viene del árabe al-jabr.
algorithm
A set of steps to solve a problem. The word comes from the name of al-Khwarizmi.
algoritmo: Un conjunto de pasos para resolver un problema. La palabra viene del nombre de al-Juarismi.
cultural diffusion
The spread of ideas, inventions and customs from one group of people to another.
difusión cultural: La propagación de ideas, inventos y costumbres de un grupo de personas a otro.

Challenge: explain your thinking

Trace the diffusion

Draw a simple map with arrows that shows how the numbers with zero traveled from India to Baghdad and then to Europe. Next to each arrow, write what helped the idea travel (for example, translation, paper or trade).

Sigue la difusión. Dibuja un mapa sencillo con flechas que muestre cómo viajaron los números con el cero desde la India hasta Bagdad y después a Europa. Junto a cada flecha, escribe qué ayudó a que viajara la idea (por ejemplo, la traducción, el papel o el comercio).

Answer in your notebook or talk it over with a classmate. Use evidence from the story.

Responde en tu cuaderno o coméntalo con un compañero. Usa evidencia del cuento.

Check your thinking

India to Baghdad: scholars translated books from India into Arabic, and al-Khwarizmi wrote about the Indian numbers. Baghdad to Europe: his books were translated into Latin, and merchants who traded around the Mediterranean learned the new numbers. Paper made the books cheaper to copy at every step.

Go further

Some people say that a culture is stronger when it learns from others. Use the story of the House of Wisdom to agree or disagree, with one piece of evidence.

Para ir más lejos. Hay quien dice que una cultura se hace más fuerte cuando aprende de otras. Usa la historia de la Casa de la Sabiduría para estar de acuerdo o en desacuerdo, con una evidencia.

Check your thinking

Example: Agree. Baghdad became a world center of learning because its scholars translated and improved ideas from Greece, Persia and India; al-Khwarizmi built on the Indian numbers to write about algebra. A strong answer could also disagree, if it gives evidence, for example that ideas can be lost when only one language is used.

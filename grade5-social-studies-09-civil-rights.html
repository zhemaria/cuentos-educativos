The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

The school choir was getting ready for an assembly in February. Mr. Okafor sat at the piano. "Today we'll learn a very special song," he said. "It's called 'Lift Every Voice and Sing.' It was written in 1900, and it was first sung by five hundred schoolchildren in Florida, kids just like you."

"Why is it special?" asked Salma. "Because many people call it the Black national ," said Mr. Okafor. "People sang it in churches, schools and meetings during the , when they were fighting for equal rights."

"What were they fighting against?" asked Sophia. "In many places, especially in the South, segregation laws kept Black people apart," the teacher said. "On city buses in Montgomery, Alabama, Black passengers had to sit in the back and give up their seats to white passengers."

"In December 1955, Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat, and she was arrested. Then the Black community of Montgomery started a : for 381 days, they refused to ride the buses." People walked miles to work, shared cars and organized rides.

"Did it work?" asked Salma. "Yes. In 1956, the Supreme Court decided that segregation on the buses was against the Constitution." "The boycott made the problem visible," said Mr. Okafor. "Then the courts acted. That's how a public problem can turn into a change."

Over the next years, people marched, wrote letters, gave speeches and voted. "In 1964, Congress passed the Civil Rights Act, which made segregation in public places illegal. In 1965, the Voting Rights Act protected the right to vote." Each came after years of pressure from ordinary people.

"Did Dr. King ever come to Chicago?" asked Sophia. "He did," said Mr. Okafor. "In 1966, he moved into an apartment on the West Side to work for . In Chicago, many Black families were not allowed to rent or buy homes in certain neighborhoods."

"They marched into those neighborhoods, and some angry crowds threw rocks at them," he said. "But they kept going. In 1968, Congress passed the Fair Housing Act, which said that people could not be refused a home because of their race."

The choir started to sing. Salma's voice shook a little at first, and then it got stronger. When they finished, Mr. Okafor was quiet for a moment. "Do you know what's special about you singing it?" he asked. "Kids sang it first, more than a hundred years ago. And kids are still singing it."

Pause to connect

🎵 Music · MU:Cn11.1.5

Read the first lines of the song aloud, slowly and with a strong voice.

Lee en voz alta los primeros versos de la canción, despacio y con voz fuerte.

Lift ev'ry voice and sing
Till earth and heaven ring,
Ring with the harmonies of Liberty;
Let our rejoicing rise
High as the list'ning skies,
Let it resound loud as the rolling sea.

“Lift Every Voice and Sing,” words by James Weldon Johnson, music by J. Rosamond Johnson, 1900 (public domain).

Good to know: The words ask everyone to sing together, so loud that the sound reaches the sky and is as strong as the sea.

Para saber más: La letra pide a todos que canten juntos, tan fuerte que el sonido llegue al cielo y sea tan poderoso como el mar.

  2. Which words in these lines make you feel big and strong? Why do you think people sang this song before a march?¿Qué palabras de estos versos te hacen sentir grande y fuerte? ¿Por qué crees que la gente cantaba esta canción antes de una marcha?

Think about it, then answer in your notebook or talk it over with someone at home.

Check your thinking

A movement needs many people working together, so "every voice" means everyone counts. Words like ring, rise, high, loud and rolling sea are big and powerful. Singing together before a march gave people courage and made them feel united. Music connects to the history and feelings of its time.

Glossary

civil rights
The rights that every citizen should have, like voting, going to school and being treated equally.
derechos civiles: Los derechos que debe tener todo ciudadano, como votar, ir a la escuela y recibir un trato igual.
boycott
When people refuse to buy or use something, to protest an unfair situation.
boicot: Cuando las personas se niegan a comprar o usar algo, para protestar contra una situación injusta.
movement
Many people working together over time to change something.
movimiento: Muchas personas trabajando juntas durante un tiempo para cambiar algo.
law
A rule made by the government that everyone must follow.
ley: Una regla hecha por el gobierno que todos deben cumplir.
fair housing
The right to rent or buy a home anywhere, no matter your race or background.
vivienda justa: El derecho a alquilar o comprar una casa en cualquier lugar, sin importar tu raza u origen.
anthem
A song that a group sings to show pride and unity.
himno: Una canción que canta un grupo para mostrar orgullo y unión.

Challenge: explain your thinking

From problem to law

Choose the Montgomery bus boycott or the Chicago fair housing marches. Explain the problem, what people did about it and what law or decision came after. Why do you think it took so long?

Del problema a la ley. Elige el boicot de autobuses de Montgomery o las marchas por la vivienda justa en Chicago. Explica el problema, qué hizo la gente y qué ley o decisión llegó después. ¿Por qué crees que tardó tanto?

Check your thinking

Montgomery: the problem was segregation on buses; people boycotted the buses for 381 days; in 1956 the Supreme Court decided bus segregation was unconstitutional. Chicago: Black families were refused homes in many neighborhoods; people marched in 1966; the Fair Housing Act came in 1968. It took long because many people in power did not want to change, so ordinary people had to keep pushing.

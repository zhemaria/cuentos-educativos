It was the first Tuesday of the month, and at ten in the morning the outdoor warning sirens began to wail. Elías jumped out of his seat. "Relax," said Ms. Rivera. "Chicago tests its sirens on the first Tuesday of every month. But today, let's talk about why we have them."

She put a map of the United States on the screen, covered with red dots. "Each dot is a from past years," she said. "Look at the pattern. Many tornadoes happen in the central United States, including Illinois, and most of them happen in spring and early summer."

"Data from the past helps scientists know where and when to be most careful," said Sophia. "Right," said Ms. Rivera. "Tornadoes are a . We can't stop them, but data can help us get ready."

Then she showed a colorful map. "This is ," she said. "It sends out radio waves that bounce off raindrops. Modern radar can even tell which way the wind inside a storm is moving. When forecasters see wind spinning in a storm, they know a tornado might form."

"So there are two kinds of messages," said Ms. Rivera. "A means the weather could make tornadoes, so be ready. A means a tornado has been seen or found on radar, so take shelter now." Daniel wrote it down: Watch = get ready. Warning = go now.

"How much time do people get?" asked Daniel. "On average, a warning gives people a few minutes, often around ten," said Ms. Rivera. "Fifty years ago, many tornadoes came with no warning at all. Better radar, better and alerts on phones have saved many lives."

That afternoon, the school held its tornado drill. Everyone walked to the inside hallway, away from windows, knelt facing the wall and covered the backs of their heads. "Why the hallway?" whispered Sophia. "Because it's in the middle of the building, on the lowest floor, with no glass," said Ms. Rivera. "Engineers design buildings and safe rooms using what we've learned from past storms."

When the drill ended, Elías stood up and brushed off his knees. "So the sirens aren't scary," he said. "They're like a friend yelling, Hey, pay attention!" "Exactly," said Ms. Rivera. "Science can't stop the storm, but it can give you time."

Glossary

natural hazard
A natural event, like a tornado, flood or earthquake, that can hurt people or damage property.
peligro natural: Un fenómeno natural, como un tornado, una inundación o un terremoto, que puede herir a las personas o dañar las propiedades.
tornado
A spinning column of air that reaches from a storm cloud down to the ground.
tornado: Una columna de aire que gira y baja desde una nube de tormenta hasta el suelo.
forecast
A prediction of what will probably happen, based on data.
pronóstico: Una predicción de lo que probablemente pasará, basada en datos.
radar
A technology that sends out radio waves to find rain and to see how the wind is moving inside a storm.
radar: Una tecnología que envía ondas de radio para encontrar la lluvia y ver cómo se mueve el viento dentro de una tormenta.
tornado watch
A message that says the weather could produce tornadoes, so people should be ready.
vigilancia de tornado: Un mensaje que dice que el tiempo podría producir tornados, así que hay que estar preparados.
tornado warning
A message that says a tornado has been seen or found on radar, so people must take shelter now.
aviso de tornado: Un aviso que dice que se vio un tornado o que el radar lo detectó, así que hay que refugiarse ya.

1. Tips for using the stories

These are the small things that make the biggest difference, and the ones that are easiest to forget on a busy day. They work for both books.

Before reading: give the story a purpose

  • Look at the picture together for two minutes before anyone reads. Ask: What do you see? What do you think? What do you wonder?
  • Teach two or three of the science or social studies words first. Each story lists them at the top, and the glossary opens with one tap.
  • Write one question on the board that the story will answer. Students read to find the answer, not just to finish.

Antes de leer: mirar la ilustración dos minutos (¿qué ves?, ¿qué piensas?, ¿qué te preguntas?), enseñar dos o tres palabras clave y escribir en la pizarra una pregunta que el cuento va a contestar.

During reading: slow down

  • Read the story aloud first, or let Lumi read it with the voice button. Then students read it again on their own or with a partner. A second reading is where comprehension happens.
  • Stop at the pause to connect and at the questions. They are not interruptions: they are the lesson.
  • One story per session is enough (20 to 30 minutes). Do not assign all twelve at once; follow the order of your class units.

Durante la lectura: leer primero en voz alta (o con la voz de Lumi) y luego que lo relean solos o en pareja; detenerse en las pausas y preguntas; un cuento por sesión.

Questions: wait, then listen

  • After you ask a question, count silently to five before calling on anyone. Most hands go up after the third second.
  • Use turn and talk: every student tells a partner first, then a few share with the class. Everybody thinks, not only the fastest.
  • Ask students to choose an answer before they tap it. The button confirms what they already said.

Preguntas: esperar cinco segundos antes de dar la palabra; que todos lo digan primero a un compañero; que elijan la respuesta antes de tocarla.

«Ayuda en español»: a bridge, not a replacement

  • The button translates instructions, questions, the glossary and the challenge. The story itself stays in English on purpose.
  • Suggested routine: first reading in English, then turn the help on to check understanding, then a final reading without it.
  • Turn the help off little by little during the year. Celebrate the day a student no longer needs it.
  • Spanish at home is an asset. Invite families to talk about the story in the language they speak best.

La «Ayuda en español» es un puente, no un sustituto: primera lectura en inglés, luego la ayuda para comprobar, y una última lectura sin ella. Retirarla poco a poco durante el año. El español en casa es una fortaleza.

Universal Design for Learning: let every student in

  • Show students the reading supports on the first day: bigger or smaller text (A+ / A−), high contrast, the voice and printing.
  • Accept more than one way to answer the challenge: writing, drawing a model, explaining out loud or recording a short audio.
  • Give sentence starters for the challenge: I think… because… The evidence is…
  • For students without a device, print the story with the «Print or save as PDF» button.

Diseño Universal para el Aprendizaje: enseñar los apoyos el primer día (A+/A−, alto contraste, voz, imprimir); aceptar varias formas de contestar el reto (escribir, dibujar, decirlo en voz alta); dar inicios de oración; imprimir el cuento para quien no tiene tableta.

The challenge: claim, evidence, reasoning

  • Every story ends with «Challenge: explain your thinking». Ask for three parts: a claim (what I think), evidence (what I saw or read) and reasoning (why the evidence supports the claim).
  • Use the challenge as an exit ticket. Two or three good sentences are worth more than a long paragraph.
  • Keep the answers in a folder. In May, students compare their first and last answers and see how much they grew.

El reto: pedir afirmación, evidencia y razonamiento. Usarlo como boleto de salida y guardar las respuestas en una carpeta para comparar al final del año.

Welcome wrong ideas

  • The stories often show a character with a wrong idea (a cousin, a friend). That is on purpose: wrong ideas are the starting point of science and history.
  • Before the reading, ask students what they think. Write their ideas on the board without correcting them, and come back to the list after the story.
  • Each activity below has a watch for note with the most common misunderstanding.

Recibir las ideas equivocadas: anotar lo que piensan antes de leer, sin corregir, y volver a la lista después del cuento. Cada actividad trae una nota con la confusión más común.

Hands-on activities: safety first

  • Wear safety goggles for anything that can splash. The adult handles hot water, heat, flames and sharp tools.
  • Check food allergies before using food (sugar, flour, gumdrops, bread). Scientists never taste the materials, even when they are food.
  • Keep decomposition bags and bottles sealed and throw them away closed.
  • Every activity can also be done as a teacher demonstration or with a video if materials or time are short.

Seguridad: gafas cuando algo pueda salpicar; el adulto maneja el agua caliente, el calor y lo filoso; revisar alergias antes de usar alimentos; nunca probar los materiales; las bolsas de descomposición se botan cerradas.

Beyond the classroom

  • Share the address www.lumi-educa.com with families. The stories work on a phone, a tablet or a computer, and the voice can read them aloud.
  • Story 12 reviews the whole year. Use it in May as a celebration and as a review before the final assessment.
  • Revisit favorite stories. Children love rereading, and every new reading finds something new.

Fuera del salón: compartir www.lumi-educa.com con las familias; usar el cuento 12 como repaso y celebración de fin de año; releer los cuentos favoritos.

2. Science

Each story has a hands-on activity with classroom materials, questions to talk about and a note on the most common misunderstanding. The activities are optional and can be done before the story (to wonder) or after it (to check the idea).

Cada cuento de ciencias tiene una actividad práctica con materiales del salón, preguntas para conversar y una nota sobre la confusión más común. Se pueden hacer antes del cuento (para despertar preguntas) o después (para comprobar la idea).

1

The invisible building blocks

Atoms join to make molecules and crystals · MS-PS1-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Build molecules

Materials: gumdrops or modeling clay in three colors, toothpicks (or building bricks), a picture of a salt crystal.

  1. Agree on a color for each element (for example, white for hydrogen, red for oxygen, black for carbon, blue for nitrogen).
  2. Groups build water, carbon dioxide and the ammonia molecule of the challenge.
  3. Compare with the salt crystal: a pattern that repeats in every direction instead of separate molecules.

Discussion questions

How is a molecule different from a crystal? What can our model show, and what can it not show?

Watch for: Students may think atoms really have colors or look like gumdrops. Discuss what every model gets right and what it leaves out.

Safety: Check food allergies if you use gumdrops. Do not eat the models.

Actividad: Construir moléculas con gomitas o plastilina y palillos (agua, dióxido de carbono, amoníaco) y compararlas con un cristal de sal. Hablar de lo que el modelo muestra y lo que no.

2

The race of the melting ice pops

Thermal energy changes particle motion and state · MS-PS1-4

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Ice that does not warm up

Materials: ice, a cup of water, a thermometer, food coloring, two clear cups (cold and warm tap water), a timer.

  1. Measure the temperature of a cup of ice water every two minutes while the ice melts. Notice that it stays near 0 °C until the ice is gone.
  2. Put one drop of food coloring in cold water and one in warm water at the same time. Compare how fast it spreads.
  3. Students draw the particles in each cup and use arrows to show how fast they move, as in the challenge.

Discussion questions

If we keep adding thermal energy, why does the temperature of the ice water not go up while the ice melts? Why does the color spread faster in warm water?

Watch for: Many students think that the temperature always goes up when something gets heat. During melting, the energy goes into changing the state.

Safety: Use warm tap water, never boiling water.

Actividad: El hielo que no se calienta. Se mide la temperatura del agua con hielo mientras se derrite (se queda cerca de 0 °C) y se compara cómo se reparte el colorante en agua fría y tibia.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): MS-PS1-4. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

3

The bike that turned orange

Chemical reactions make new substances · MS-PS1-2

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Before and after

Materials: steel wool (no soap), two jars, vinegar, water, a magnet, milk, a strainer, gloves.

  1. Dip one piece of steel wool in vinegar and leave another one dry. Put each in a jar and observe them for three to five days.
  2. Record the properties before and after in a table: color, texture, whether it is attracted to a magnet.
  3. As a quick extra, add a little vinegar to warm milk and strain it: the curds are a new substance.

Discussion questions

Which evidence shows that a new substance was made? How is that different from ice melting?

Watch for: Students often call every change a reaction. Melting or cutting changes the shape or the state, not the substance.

Safety: Gloves for the steel wool. Goggles when you pour vinegar.

Actividad: Antes y después. Lana de acero con vinagre y sin vinagre durante varios días; se anotan sus propiedades antes y después. Como extra, leche tibia con vinagre forma cuajada: una sustancia nueva.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): MS-PS1-2. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

4

The great sled race

Forces and mass change motion · MS-PS2-2

Open the story

Hands-on activity: A fair test with toy cars

Materials: toy cars, metal washers, tape, a ramp or a rubber band launcher, a tape measure.

  1. Plan the test like the challenge: the same push for every car, and only the mass changes.
  2. Launch the car without washers and then with two and four washers taped on. Repeat each launch three times and measure the distance.
  3. Make a table and a graph, and write a conclusion with evidence.

Discussion questions

Which variable did you change? Which ones did you keep the same? Why do we repeat each launch three times?

Watch for: A common idea is that heavier objects always go faster. With the same force, more mass speeds up less.

Safety: Launch the cars away from people.

Actividad: Una prueba justa con carritos. El mismo empujón y distinta masa (arandelas): se repite tres veces, se mide la distancia y se saca una conclusión con evidencia.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): MS-PS2-2. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

5

Keep the tamales warm

Designing to slow down heat transfer · MS-PS3-3

Open the story

Hands-on activity: The warm cup challenge

Materials: identical cups, warm tap water, thermometers, foil, cloth, newspaper, foam, rubber bands, a timer.

  1. Agree on the criteria (the water stays as warm as possible for 30 minutes) and the constraints (materials, size, time).
  2. Each group designs and builds a container for one cup. The adult pours the same warm water into every cup.
  3. Measure the temperature every 5 minutes, graph the results, improve the design and test again.

Discussion questions

Which materials are good insulators? How would you change your design to keep an ice pop frozen?

Watch for: Students often think insulators "make heat" or "make cold". They only slow down the transfer of thermal energy.

Safety: The adult pours the warm water. Never use boiling water.

Actividad: El reto del vaso tibio. Los grupos diseñan un recipiente con materiales aislantes para que el agua tibia se enfríe lo menos posible en 30 minutos; se mide cada 5 minutos, se grafica y se mejora el diseño.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): MS-PS3-3. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

6

The secret under the microscope

All living things are made of cells · MS-LS1-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Onion skin under the microscope

Materials: microscopes or strong magnifiers, slides, an onion, a dropper, water, a tomato.

  1. Peel a very thin layer from inside an onion, place it flat on a slide with a drop of water and look at it.
  2. Students draw what they see and label the cell wall and the nucleus.
  3. Look at the flesh of a tomato. Answer the challenge: is it made of cells too?

Discussion questions

What do all living things have in common? How did microscopes change what scientists knew?

Watch for: Students may think that soft or juicy things are not made of cells, or that only animals have cells. Every living thing is made of cells.

Safety: The adult cuts the onion. Handle the glass slides with care. If there are no microscopes, use the photos in the story.

Actividad: Piel de cebolla al microscopio. Se observa una capa finísima de cebolla y la pulpa de un tomate, se dibujan las células y se señalan la pared celular y el núcleo.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): MS-LS1-1. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

7

The body that ran a race

Body systems work together · MS-LS1-3

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Pulse and breathing

Materials: a timer, a data table.

  1. Students count their pulse and their breaths for 30 seconds while they are resting.
  2. After one minute of movement (marching in place, jumping jacks), they count again.
  3. They compare the data and explain which systems worked together and how, with evidence.

Discussion questions

Why did your heart and your breathing speed up together? Which system sent the message?

Watch for: Students tend to study each system on its own. The point is that they work as a team.

Safety: Offer gentle movements as an option. A student with a health condition can be the data recorder.

Actividad: Pulso y respiración. Se cuentan el pulso y las respiraciones en reposo y después de un minuto de movimiento, y se explica qué sistemas trabajaron juntos. Ofrecer movimientos suaves como opción.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): MS-LS1-3. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

8

Who eats whom in the lake

Energy flows and matter cycles in an ecosystem · MS-LS2-3

Open the story

Hands-on activity: The lake web in yarn

Materials: name cards (phytoplankton, zooplankton, small fish, big fish, bird, decomposers), a ball of yarn.

  1. In a circle, each student holds a card. The yarn goes from the living thing that is eaten to the one that eats it: the arrow follows the energy.
  2. Ask one living thing to let go of the yarn. Who feels it? What happens to the others?
  3. Students draw the food web of a Chicago park, as in the challenge.

Discussion questions

Why do arrows in a food web point toward the eater? What happens to matter when a fish dies?

Watch for: Students often draw the arrows backward (from the eater to the food). The arrow shows where the energy goes.

Actividad: La red del lago con estambre. En círculo, el estambre va del que es comido al que come (la flecha sigue la energía); se suelta un ser vivo y se observa qué pasa con los demás.

9

The moon in a dark classroom

The Earth-sun-moon system · MS-ESS1-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Moon phases with a lamp

Materials: a lamp without a shade, a white foam ball on a pencil for each student, a dark room, a moon calendar.

  1. As in the story, the lamp is the sun and each student's head is Earth. Hold the ball at arm's length and turn slowly in place.
  2. Watch how the lit part of the ball changes: new moon, crescent, quarter, full.
  3. For a month, students draw the moon they see each night in a moon journal.

Discussion questions

If half of the moon is always lit, why do we see different shapes? Why can a lunar eclipse happen only at full moon?

Watch for: Most students believe the phases are caused by Earth's shadow. Earth's shadow only causes lunar eclipses.

Safety: The bulb gets hot: do not touch it. Never look directly at the sun.

Actividad: Fases de la luna con una lámpara. La lámpara es el Sol y la cabeza de cada estudiante es la Tierra; al girar con una bola en la mano se ven las fases. Llevar un diario de la luna durante un mes.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): MS-ESS1-1. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

10

The journey of a raindrop

The sun and gravity drive the water cycle · MS-ESS2-4

Open the story

Hands-on activity: A water cycle in a bowl

Materials: a clear bowl, plastic wrap, warm tap water, ice cubes, a small cup, a lamp or a sunny window.

  1. As in the story, pour warm water into the bowl, put the small cup in the middle, cover with plastic wrap and put ice on top.
  2. Wait 15 to 20 minutes. Observe the drops that form under the plastic and fall into the cup.
  3. Students draw the model with Lake Michigan, a cloud and rain on Chicago, and label where the sun's energy and gravity act.

Discussion questions

Where does the energy for evaporation come from? What makes the drops fall?

Watch for: Students often think that clouds are made of water vapor, which is invisible. Clouds are tiny drops of liquid water.

Safety: Use warm tap water, never boiling water.

Actividad: Un ciclo del agua en un tazón. Agua tibia, plástico encima y hielo arriba: se forman gotas que caen. Luego se dibuja el modelo con el lago Michigan y se señala dónde actúan el Sol y la gravedad.

11

When the sirens sang

Forecasting natural hazards and staying safe · MS-ESS3-2

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Read the tornado data

Materials: a table of tornadoes by month in Illinois (from the National Weather Service), graph paper, the school safety plan.

  1. Groups make a bar graph of tornadoes by month and look for the pattern.
  2. Compare a tornado watch with a tornado warning and decide what the school should do in each case.
  3. Review the school's tornado drill: where to go and how to protect yourself.

Discussion questions

How does the data help a town get ready? What is the difference between a watch and a warning?

Watch for: Students sometimes think scientists can tell exactly when and where a tornado will touch down. Forecasts give probabilities and early warnings.

Safety: Some students may feel scared by this topic. Keep the focus on preparation and on how technology keeps people safe.

Actividad: Leer los datos de tornados. Se grafica cuántos tornados hay por mes en Illinois, se compara una vigilancia con una advertencia y se repasa el simulacro de la escuela. Cuidar a quienes sientan miedo.

3. Social studies

Each story has a pause to connect that a child can do alone on a tablet: look at a work of art from the time or read lines from a public domain poem. The activities in this guide are for the whole class or small groups. They are optional.

The arts connections follow Anchor Standard 11 (Connecting) of the Illinois Arts Learning Standards. The stories use only well-established historical facts and do not put invented words in the mouths of real people. When students write from the point of view of a historical person, present it clearly as their own creative writing.

The Illinois Social Science Standards for middle school are written for the grade band 6–8, not for Grade 6 alone. Many history and civics standards come in three levels of complexity: LC (Less Complex), MdC (Moderately Complex) and MC (More Complex). The challenges in this collection aim mainly at the LC level. The optional Go further questions offer chances for more complex reasoning; whether a task matches MdC or MC must be judged task by task. A harder question does not, by itself, show an MdC standard.

In each chapter note below, keep three things apart: the official description of the standard, the goal as adapted to the story, and the evidence the student produces (the answer to the challenge).

Cada cuento de estudios sociales tiene una pausa para conectar con las artes (una obra, un poema o una canción de la época) y aquí una actividad de grupo, preguntas para conversar y el estándar de Illinois.

1

The box from 1976

Primary and secondary sources · SS.6-8.H.3.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Class time capsule

VA:Cn11.1.6

Each group brings or draws three objects for a class time capsule and writes a museum label for each one. On the back of each label, the group writes whether the object is a primary or a secondary source and one thing a future historian could NOT learn from it. Seal the labels in a box to open at the end of the year.

Discussion questions

Who made this source, when and why? Why might two people from the same year describe it in different ways?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

In a museum, every artifact has a small label next to it. The label tells visitors what the object is, when it was made and why it matters. Imagine that the cassette tape from the time capsule is now in a museum.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.6-8.H.3.LC: classify the kinds of historical sources (primary and secondary) and explain what each one can and cannot tell us about the past. The challenge lets students show SS.6-8.H.3.LC; the optional “Go further” question invites deeper analysis. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.6: analyze how art reflects changing times, traditions, resources and cultural uses.

4

Everybody votes?

Democracy in Athens and today · SS.6-8.CV.4.LC · SS.6-8.H.2.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Assembly vote · Design a class coin

VA:Cn11.1.6

Hold a direct vote on a real class choice. Then repeat it, but this time only students born in the first half of the year may vote. Make clear that the second vote is only a simulation with no real consequences: the decision that counts is the first vote, in which everyone took part. Discuss how the excluded students felt, and connect it to who could vote in Athens. Finally, groups design a class coin with one symbol on each side, like the owl and Athena, and explain what the symbols say about the class.

Discussion questions

How was Athens a democracy, and how was it not? How have ideas about who can vote changed over time?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at a silver coin made in Athens around 450 years before the Common Era. Athenians used coins like this to buy and sell.

5

Rules on the wall

The Twelve Tables and written laws · SS.6-8.CV.2.LC

Open the story

Group activity: Our twelve tables · Arch challenge

VA:Cn11.1.6

The class writes and votes on twelve short rules, posts them on the wall and signs them. As an extension, groups build an arch and a flat bridge of the same width out of paper or cardboard, then test which one holds more coins, to see why Roman builders used arches in aqueducts like the Pont du Gard.

Discussion questions

Why did the plebeians want the laws written down? Can a written law still be unfair?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the Pont du Gard, a Roman aqueduct bridge in what is now southern France. The Romans built it about 2,000 years ago to carry water to the city of Nîmes.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). 4. The stories in one page · Grade 6

Before

  • Look at the picture for 2 minutes: see, think, wonder.
  • Teach 2 or 3 key words.
  • Write the question the story will answer.

During

  • Read aloud first; then students reread.
  • Stop at the pause and the questions.
  • Count to 5 before calling on anyone. Turn and talk.

After

  • Challenge: claim, evidence, reasoning.
  • Accept drawings and oral answers.
  • Keep the answer as an exit ticket.

Always

  • «Ayuda en español» after the first English reading; withdraw it little by little.
  • Show the supports: A+/A−, high contrast, voice, print.
  • Goggles, no tasting, the adult handles heat.

Antes: ilustración 2 minutos, 2 o 3 palabras clave, la pregunta del cuento. Durante: leer en voz alta y releer, detenerse en las pausas, esperar 5 segundos. Después: afirmación, evidencia y razonamiento; aceptar dibujos y respuestas orales. Siempre: la ayuda en español después de la primera lectura en inglés; mostrar los apoyos; seguridad.

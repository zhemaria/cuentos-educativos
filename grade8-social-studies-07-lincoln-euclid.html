The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

Ms. Novak put an old book on her desk. On the open page was a triangle with letters at its corners. "This is a copy of Euclid's Elements, a Greek geometry book more than 2,000 years old," she said. "And a famous lawyer from Springfield, Illinois, studied it in his free time. His name was Abraham Lincoln."

"Lincoln went to school for less than a year in total," she said. "Later, while studying law, he kept seeing the word , and he realized he didn't really know what it meant. So he decided to study the first six books of Euclid until he could prove any in them."

"In a short autobiography he wrote in 1860, he said that he had studied and nearly mastered the six books of Euclid after he had served in Congress," said Ms. Novak. "Friends remembered him reading geometry by candlelight while traveling the court circuit, while the other lawyers snored." The class laughed.

"What does geometry have to do with being a lawyer?" asked Selena. "In Euclid, you start from a few , basic ideas everyone accepts," said Ms. Novak. "Then you prove each new step from the steps before. Lincoln wanted his arguments to work the same way: start from something everyone agrees on, and move step by step to a conclusion."

"In 1858, Lincoln ran for the United States Senate against Stephen Douglas," she said. "They held seven in seven Illinois towns: Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy and Alton. Thousands of people came to listen." "What did they argue about?" asked Elías. "Mainly about slavery: whether it should be allowed to spread into the new territories in the West," said Ms. Novak.

"Who won?" asked Daniel. "Douglas kept his seat in the Senate," said Ms. Novak. "But newspapers printed the debates, and Lincoln became known across the country. Two years later, he was elected president." "Later, Lincoln wrote that the principles of Jefferson were the definitions and axioms of a free society," she added. "He was using the language of geometry to make a political ."

Then Ms. Novak gave the class a challenge. Athena and Daniel stood at two desks, like podiums, and argued whether the school day should start later. "Remember Euclid," said Ms. Novak. "Start from something both sides accept, and support every step with ." Athena began: "We all agree that students should learn as much as possible…"

When the bell rang, Israel closed the geometry book. "So a proof and a good argument are cousins," he said. Sophia wrote in her notebook: Saying it isn't proving it. Start from what we share, and build one step at a time.

Glossary

demonstrate
To prove that something is true, step by step, with reasons that no one can reasonably deny.
demostrar: Probar que algo es verdad, paso a paso, con razones que nadie puede negar con fundamento.
proposition
A statement to be proved, like a theorem in geometry.
proposición: Un enunciado que hay que demostrar, como un teorema de geometría.
axiom
A basic idea accepted as true, used as the starting point of a proof.
axioma: Una idea básica que se acepta como verdadera y sirve de punto de partida para una demostración.
debate
A public discussion in which people present arguments for opposite positions.
debate: Una discusión pública en la que las personas presentan argumentos a favor de posturas opuestas.
argument
A claim supported by evidence and reasons.
argumento: Una afirmación apoyada con evidencia y razones.
evidence
Facts, documents and examples that support a claim.
evidencia: Hechos, documentos y ejemplos que apoyan una afirmación.

  3. CQuestion C. How is a Euclid proof like a good argument?

Challenge: explain your thinking

Build an argument

Choose a question about your school, like whether lunch should be longer. Write an argument the way Lincoln learned from Euclid: one starting point that everyone accepts, two steps supported by evidence, and a conclusion.

Construye un argumento. Elige una pregunta sobre tu escuela, por ejemplo si el almuerzo debería durar más. Escribe un argumento como Lincoln aprendió de Euclides: un punto de partida que todos acepten, dos pasos apoyados con evidencia y una conclusión.

Answer in your notebook or talk it over with a classmate. Use evidence from the story.

Responde en tu cuaderno o coméntalo con un compañero. Usa evidencia del cuento.

Check your thinking

Example. Starting point: we all agree that students need energy to learn in the afternoon. Step 1: many students don't finish their food in 20 minutes; in a class survey, 18 of 25 said they leave food on the tray. Step 2: students who don't eat enough have less energy; the nurse sees more students with headaches after lunch. Conclusion: a longer lunch would help students learn better in the afternoon. Each step rests on the one before it.

Go further

Lincoln and Douglas were running for the U.S. Senate, but voters didn't choose senators directly in 1858. Find out who chose senators then and which amendment changed that.

Para ir más lejos. Lincoln y Douglas competían por un escaño en el Senado de los EE. UU., pero en 1858 los votantes no elegían directamente a los senadores. Averigua quién los elegía entonces y qué enmienda cambió eso.

Check your thinking

In 1858, senators were chosen by the state legislatures. The voters elected members of the Illinois legislature, and the legislature chose Douglas. The Seventeenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, gave voters the right to elect their senators directly.

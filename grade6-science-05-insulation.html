The story

Salma's grandmother made two dozen tamales for the sixth grade potluck. The problem was the schedule. The tamales would come out of the steamer at 11:00, but the potluck didn't start until 1:00. "Cold tamales are a tragedy," said Salma.

Ms. Rivera turned the problem into a design challenge. "Your : the tamales must still be warm, above 60 degrees Celsius, after two hours. Your : you can only use materials from this table, and the container must fit in a backpack."

On the table were a metal tray, foam cups, a wool scarf, aluminum foil, newspaper, towels and a small foam cooler. "First, why do warm things get cold?" asked Ms. Rivera. "Thermal energy moves on its own from warmer things to cooler things," said Daniel. "The hot tamales give their energy to the cooler air and to anything they touch. That's ."

Athena touched the metal tray and then a foam cup. "The metal feels colder, even though they're both at room temperature." "That's because metal is a good ," said Ms. Rivera. "It pulls thermal energy out of your warm hand quickly by . The foam is an , so it slows the energy down."

So the team built a design. They wrapped the pot in a towel, put it in the foam cooler, and stuffed crumpled newspaper around it. "Crumpled paper traps lots of tiny pockets of air," said Salma. "Trapped air is a great insulator, because it can't move around and carry the energy away." Before the test, Daniel checked the other constraint. He slid the cooler into his backpack and pulled the zipper closed. "It fits," he said. "Now let's see if it meets the criteria."

Then they tested it. They put a thermometer in one tamale inside the cooler and another thermometer in one tamale left on the metal tray. Every half hour, they wrote down both temperatures. After two hours, the tamale on the tray was at 28 degrees. The one in the cooler was still at 61.

"Success!" shouted Daniel. But Ms. Rivera asked one more question. "How could you make it even better?" Athena looked closely at the cooler. "The lid didn't close tightly. Warm air escaped there. Next time we can seal the lid with tape and test again." "That's exactly what engineers do," said Ms. Rivera. "Test, improve, test again."

At one o'clock, Salma opened the cooler, and steam rose into the room. The whole class lined up. Ms. Rivera took the last tamale and closed her eyes. "This," she said, "is the most delicious science experiment I have ever graded."

Glossary

thermal energy transfer
The movement of thermal energy from something warmer to something colder.
transferencia de energía térmica: El paso de la energía térmica de algo más caliente a algo más frío.
conduction
The transfer of thermal energy between things that are touching.
conducción: La transferencia de energía térmica entre cosas que se tocan.
conductor
A material that lets thermal energy move through it easily, like metal.
conductor: Un material que deja pasar la energía térmica con facilidad, como el metal.
insulator
A material that slows down the transfer of thermal energy, like foam, wool or trapped air.
aislante: Un material que hace más lenta la transferencia de energía térmica, como la espuma (unicel), la lana o el aire atrapado.
criteria
The things a design must do to be successful.
criterios: Lo que debe lograr un diseño para tener éxito.
constraints
The limits a design must follow, like time, money or materials.
restricciones: Los límites que debe respetar un diseño, como el tiempo, el dinero o los materiales.

Challenge: explain your thinking

Design and test

Now design the opposite: a container that keeps an ice pop frozen for as long as possible on a hot day. Which materials would you use, and why? How would you test whether your design works?

Diseña y prueba. Ahora diseña lo contrario: un recipiente que mantenga congelada una paleta el mayor tiempo posible en un día caluroso. ¿Qué materiales usarías y por qué? ¿Cómo probarías si tu diseño funciona?

Answer in your notebook or talk it over with a classmate. Use evidence from the story.

Responde en tu cuaderno o coméntalo con un compañero. Usa evidencia del cuento.

Check your thinking

Use insulators, like foam, wool, towels or crumpled paper with trapped air, and a tight lid, so thermal energy from the hot air moves into the ice pop slowly. Avoid metal on the outside. To test it, put one ice pop in your container and another out in the open, and check both every 15 minutes. Insulators slow transfer in both directions: they keep hot things hot and cold things cold.

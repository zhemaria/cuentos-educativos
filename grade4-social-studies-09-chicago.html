The story

One Sunday, Sophia's mom took Sophia, Daniel and Athena for a bike ride along a quiet old canal southwest of Chicago. Ducks paddled among the leaves. "This canal changed Chicago's history," said Sophia's mom.

"In 1837, when Chicago became a city, its was only about 4,000 people," she explained. "That's smaller than lots of towns today." "It would fit in a small stadium!" said Daniel.

"In 1848, the Illinois and Michigan opened. It joined the Chicago River to the Illinois River, so Lake Michigan was connected to the Mississippi River." "Jolliet's idea!" said Athena, who remembered the portage.

The path they were riding on ran right along the water. Long ago, mules walked along this path, pulling the boats with ropes. The boats carried grain, lumber and passengers. Many of the workers who dug the canal were from Ireland.

That same year, 1848, the first train left Chicago. Soon more and more lines arrived, and Chicago became the most important train center in the country. Grain, cattle and lumber poured in from many places, and from Chicago they were shipped to other cities. The city was a giant hub of .

Athena hopped off her bike and drew a graph in her sketchbook. "By 1870, Chicago had almost 300,000 people," Sophia's mom told her. Athena's line shot up like a rocket.

"But in October 1871, the Great Fire burned much of the city," Sophia's mom went on. "Chicago rebuilt with brick, stone and iron, and it kept on growing. By 1890 it had more than a million people." Some of the world's first skyscrapers went up there in those years.

Over time, trains and bigger canals replaced the old canal. Today it's a place to walk and ride, and its banks hold its history. "So the canal and the trains were the ," said Daniel, "and a giant city was the ."

Sophia watched a duck glide calmly through the water where boats full of grain once passed. "Lucky duck," she said. "It doesn't have to dodge any mules."

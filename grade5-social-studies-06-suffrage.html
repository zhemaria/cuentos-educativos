The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

On Election Day, Selena and Sophia walked with their mothers to the school gym, which had become a polling place. When their mothers came out, they each had a small sticker. "I voted," Selena's mother said proudly. "My grandmother couldn't do that when she was young."

In class, Mr. Okafor wrote a word on the board: . "It means the right to vote," he said. "For most of this country's history, women could not vote in most elections. The Constitution let each state decide who could vote, and most states only let men vote."

For many years, women organized, gave speeches, wrote articles and marched. "In 1913, Illinois passed a law that let women vote for president and for some local offices," said Mr. Okafor. "It was the first state east of the Mississippi River to do it."

"That same year, in Chicago, a journalist named Ida B. Wells started the Alpha Suffrage Club," he continued. "It was the first club in Illinois for Black women who wanted the right to vote." Ida B. Wells had already spent many years writing articles against and violence against Black people.

In March 1913, thousands of women marched in a huge for suffrage in Washington, D.C. Some organizers asked the Black women to march at the back, separated from the others. "Ida B. Wells refused," said Mr. Okafor. "She waited by the side of the street, and when the Illinois group passed, she stepped in and marched with them."

"Good for her," said Sophia. Athena was already drawing the program cover from that parade: a woman on a white horse, blowing a long trumpet and carrying a banner. "Artists made posters, banners and programs to convince people," said the teacher. "Art was part of the fight."

"In 1920, the 19th to the Constitution said that no state could stop a from voting because she was a woman." Selena cheered. "So everybody could vote!" Mr. Okafor shook his head gently. "Not yet. That's the hard part of this story."

"Many Native Americans could not vote, because they were not even counted as citizens until 1924. And in many Southern states, unfair rules and threats kept most Black women and men from voting for many more years." "So the rules changed," said Selena slowly, "but not for everyone at the same time." "Exactly. Each new came because people kept pushing."

On the way home, Selena looked at the sticker her mother had given her. "When I'm eighteen, I'm going to vote in every single election," she said. Her mother smiled. "Even the small ones?" "Especially the small ones," said Selena. "Somebody marched for them."

Pause to connect

🖼️ Visual arts · VA:Cn11.1.5

Look at the cover of the official program of the 1913 suffrage procession in Washington, D.C.

Observa la portada del programa oficial del desfile por el sufragio de 1913 en Washington, D. C.

What you will see: A woman rides a white horse and blows a long trumpet. A banner hangs from the trumpet. Behind her, you can see the dome of the Capitol, where Congress meets.

Lo que verás: Una mujer monta un caballo blanco y toca una trompeta larga. De la trompeta cuelga un estandarte. Detrás de ella se ve la cúpula del Capitolio, donde se reúne el Congreso.

  1. Why do you think the artist put the Capitol behind the woman?¿Por qué crees que el artista puso el Capitolio detrás de la mujer?
  2. What feeling does the trumpet give you? What message does the picture send?¿Qué sensación te da la trompeta? ¿Qué mensaje envía la imagen?

Think about it, then answer in your notebook or talk it over with someone at home.

Check your thinking

The Capitol is where Congress makes laws, so the picture says: women are coming to Congress to ask for a new law. A trumpet is used to announce something important, so the message is strong and brave. The artist used images, not long explanations, to convince people to support suffrage.

Glossary

suffrage
The right to vote.
sufragio: El derecho al voto.
amendment
A change or addition to the Constitution.
enmienda: Un cambio o una adición a la Constitución.
citizen
A member of a country, with rights and responsibilities.
ciudadano: Un miembro de un país, con derechos y responsabilidades.
procession
A large group of people walking together in order, like a parade.
desfile: Un grupo grande de personas que camina en orden, como una procesión.
discrimination
Treating people unfairly because of who they are.
discriminación: Tratar a las personas de forma injusta por ser quienes son.
policy
A plan or rule that a government follows to solve a problem.
política pública: Un plan o una regla que sigue un gobierno para resolver un problema.

Challenge: explain your thinking

Propose a policy

Think of a problem at your school, like a crowded lunch line or a playground that some students can't use. Explain who is affected, and propose a rule or policy that would be fair for everyone. How would you convince the principal?

Propón una política. Piensa en un problema de tu escuela, como una fila del almuerzo muy larga o un patio que algunos alumnos no pueden usar. Explica a quién afecta y propón una regla o política que sea justa para todos. ¿Cómo convencerías a la directora?

Answer in your notebook or talk it over with a classmate. Use evidence from the story.

Responde en tu cuaderno o coméntalo con un compañero. Usa evidencia del cuento.

Check your thinking

Example: the swings are always taken by the same group. Policy: a sign-up schedule by grade. To convince the principal, collect evidence (count how many students never get a turn), listen to different points of view and make a poster that explains the idea clearly, just like the suffragists did.

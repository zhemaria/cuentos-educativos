Mr. Mensah dropped a soft, pale lump onto the table. Selena poked it. "It's sticky," she said. "That's natural rubber," said Mr. Mensah. "It comes from the milky sap of rubber trees, which grow in warm, rainy places. It's a . And in the 1830s, it was a big problem."

"People in the United States made rubber boots, coats and mailbags," he said. "But natural rubber has bad : in hot summers it melted into a sticky, smelly mess, and in cold winters it turned hard and cracked." Israel squeezed his bouncy ball. "Mine doesn't melt." "Because of a man named Charles Goodyear."

"Goodyear spent years trying to fix rubber," Mr. Mensah went on. "He mixed it with everything he could think of. His family was so poor that sometimes they didn't have enough to eat, and he even went to jail for his debts. But he didn't give up."

He held up a jar of yellow powder. "In 1839, as Goodyear himself told the story, he dropped a piece of rubber mixed with this, sulfur, onto a hot stove. He expected it to melt. Instead, it charred like leather, and the rest stayed firm and flexible."

"The heat caused a ," said Mr. Mensah. "Rubber is a , made of very long chains of molecules. The sulfur joined the chains together, like the rungs of a ladder. The new material didn't melt in the heat or crack in the cold." Goodyear called the process , after Vulcan, the Roman god of fire. He received a patent for it in 1844.

"Is that why tires are made of rubber?" asked Selena. "Yes," said Mr. Mensah. "Vulcanized rubber made bicycle and car tires possible, along with hoses, boots and thousands of other things." "It changed transportation and factories around the world. Goodyear never got rich, though. A tire company was named after him almost 40 years after he died."

He pointed to the table, where he had placed a plastic water bottle and a nylon jacket. "These are materials. People make them through chemical processes, starting from natural resources like oil and natural gas, instead of finding them ready-made in nature." "So synthetic doesn't mean it comes from nowhere," said Israel. "Right. It always starts with something from the Earth."

"But synthetic materials also cause problems," said Mr. Mensah. "Many plastics last for hundreds of years in landfills and oceans, and making them uses oil that won't come back." Selena looked at her water bottle. "So every new material is a trade-off," she said. "Goodyear solved one problem, and now we have to solve the next one."

Glossary

natural resource
Something from nature that people use, like water, trees, rubber sap or oil.
recurso natural: Algo de la naturaleza que la gente usa, como el agua, los árboles, la savia del caucho o el petróleo.
property
A feature of a material, like how hard, stretchy or sticky it is, or how it reacts to heat.
propiedad: Una característica de un material, como lo duro, elástico o pegajoso que es, o cómo reacciona al calor.
chemical reaction
A change in which substances rearrange their atoms to form new substances with new properties.
reacción química: Un cambio en el que las sustancias reordenan sus átomos y forman sustancias nuevas con propiedades nuevas.
vulcanization
Heating rubber with sulfur so it stays strong and stretchy in both heat and cold.
vulcanización: Calentar el caucho con azufre para que siga fuerte y elástico tanto con calor como con frío.
synthetic
Made by people through chemical processes, instead of found ready-made in nature.
sintético: Fabricado por las personas mediante procesos químicos, en lugar de encontrarse ya hecho en la naturaleza.
polymer
A substance made of very long chains of repeating molecules, like rubber, plastic or nylon.
polímero: Una sustancia formada por cadenas muy largas de moléculas que se repiten, como el caucho, el plástico o el nailon.

Example: plastic comes from oil and natural gas. It helps because it is light, cheap and waterproof, so it keeps food and medicine safe.

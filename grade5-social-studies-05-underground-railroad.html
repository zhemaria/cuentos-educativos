The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

On a cold Saturday in October, the class rode a bus to Princeton, Illinois, to visit an old white farmhouse. A park guide met them at the door. "This was the home of Owen Lovejoy," she said. "In the 1840s and 1850s, it was a stop on the ."

"A railroad under the ground?" asked Israel. "With secret trains?" The guide smiled. "It wasn't a real railroad. It was a secret network of people and safe places that helped escape from in the South and travel north."

"But Illinois was a free state," said Salma. "Why did people need to hide here?" "Because a federal law from 1850 said that people who escaped slavery could be captured, even in free states, and sent back," said the guide. "And anyone who helped them could be punished."

Owen Lovejoy was an , a person who worked to end slavery. "He and his family hid freedom seekers in their house, gave them food and helped them continue to the next safe place," said the guide. "Many traveled all the way to Canada, where they could not be captured."

"Did anyone in Chicago help?" asked Israel. "Yes," said Mr. Okafor. "John Jones and his wife, Mary Jones, were free Black people who lived in Chicago. Their home was a safe place for freedom seekers. Later, John Jones also fought to end the unfair laws, called the Black Laws, that limited the rights of Black people in Illinois. They were ended in 1865."

Back on the bus, Mr. Okafor helped them make a of events on a long strip of paper. 1850: the law that let people be captured in free states. 1850s: Lovejoy and the Joneses help freedom seekers. 1861: the Civil War begins. 1865: the 13th Amendment ends slavery in the whole country, and the Black Laws of Illinois are ended too.

"Most of the people who escaped didn't have help at the beginning," said Mr. Okafor. "They were brave, they planned in secret, and they often traveled at night. The helpers mattered, but the freedom seekers themselves were the heroes of their own stories."

"How did they talk about escaping without being caught?" asked Salma. "Some used , religious songs created by enslaved African Americans," said Mr. Okafor. "A book written about Harriet Tubman in 1869 says that she used a song called 'Go Down, Moses' as a signal."

Israel suddenly sat up straight. "Wait. There's a song with my name in it?" "The first line says 'When Israel was in Egypt's land,'" said the teacher. "In the song, Israel means the people of Israel in the Bible, who were enslaved and wanted to be free." Israel thought about it quietly. "Then it's a good song to have my name in," he said.

“Go Down, Moses,” African American spiritual, first printed in 1862 (public domain).

Good to know: In the Bible story, Moses asks the king of Egypt, called Pharaoh, to free the people of Israel. The words "Let my people go!" repeat again and again.

Para saber más: En el relato de la Biblia, Moisés le pide al rey de Egipto, el faraón, que libere al pueblo de Israel. Las palabras «Let my people go!» («¡Deja ir a mi pueblo!») se repiten una y otra vez.

  1. Why do you think enslaved people sang about a story of people who were freed?¿Por qué crees que las personas esclavizadas cantaban sobre un relato de un pueblo que fue liberado?
  2. Why does a line that repeats, like "Let my people go!", make a song powerful?¿Por qué una línea que se repite, como «Let my people go!», le da fuerza a una canción?

Think about it, then answer in your notebook or talk it over with someone at home.

Check your thinking

The Bible story was about people who were enslaved and then freed, so it gave hope and showed what enslaved people wanted: freedom. They could sing it openly because it was a religious song. A line that repeats is easy to remember and to sing together, and it makes the message stronger each time.

Glossary

slavery
When people are owned by other people and forced to work without pay or freedom.
esclavitud: Cuando unas personas son dueñas de otras y las obligan a trabajar sin paga ni libertad.
freedom seeker
A person who escaped from slavery to find freedom.
persona en busca de libertad: Una persona que escapaba de la esclavitud para encontrar la libertad.
Underground Railroad
A secret network of people and safe places that helped freedom seekers. It was not a real railroad.
Ferrocarril Subterráneo: Una red secreta de personas y lugares seguros que ayudaba a quienes huían. No era un ferrocarril de verdad.
abolitionist
A person who worked to end slavery.
abolicionista: Una persona que luchaba para acabar con la esclavitud.
spiritual
A religious song created by enslaved African Americans.
espiritual: Una canción religiosa creada por afroamericanos esclavizados.
sequence
The order in which events happened.

Challenge: explain your thinking

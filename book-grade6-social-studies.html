Chapter 1: The box from 1976

Historians learn from primary and secondary sources

Grade 6 · Social studies · SS.6-8.H.3.LC

A story about a time capsule and the different kinds of sources historians use.

Key words

The story

For the school's 100th birthday, the principal brought a dusty metal box to the library. "This time capsule was sealed by sixth graders in 1976," she said, "and today, sixth graders get to open it." Ms. Haddad smiled at her class. "Today, you are not students. You are ."

The lid creaked open. On top was a letter in neat cursive. Selena read it aloud: "Dear future students, today our class planted a tree for America's 200th birthday. I hope you still have recess." "This letter is a ," said Ms. Haddad. "It was written by someone who was there, at the time."

Daniel pulled out a small plastic rectangle with two holes and a brown ribbon inside. "What is this?" "A cassette tape," laughed Ms. Haddad. "People recorded music and voices on it." "It's also an , an object people used. Artifacts tell us how people lived, even when nobody wrote about them."

Next came a black-and-white photo of children standing in front of a brick school. Athena held it next to the window and gasped. "That's our school! But there's no gym, and the street has no trees." Ms. Haddad nodded. "A photo is a primary source too. It shows us what changed and what stayed the same."

At the bottom of the box was a thin booklet called The History of Our Neighborhood, written by a teacher in 1975. It told stories from the 1920s and 1930s. "This is a ," said Ms. Haddad. "The teacher wasn't alive in the 1920s. She studied old newspapers and interviews, and then she wrote about them."

Israel frowned. "So secondary sources are worse?" "Not worse, just different," said Ms. Haddad. "A secondary source can explain the big picture. A primary source gives you the voice of one person." Then she held up two more letters from the capsule. One boy wrote that 1976 was the best year ever. A girl wrote that her family had just moved and she missed her old friends. "Same school, same year, two different ."

"So which one is true?" asked Sophia. "Both can be true," said Ms. Haddad. "That's why historians never trust just one source. They compare many pieces of , they ask who made each one and why, and then they build an explanation."

Before the bell rang, the class decided to make their own time capsule for 2076. Daniel wanted to put in his phone. "You can't," said Selena. "You need it." So he wrote a letter instead: Dear future students, we also hope you still have recess.

The story

At the museum of ancient cultures, a stone bull with wings and a bearded human face stood taller than two basketball players. "He guarded a palace in Mesopotamia about 2,700 years ago," said Ms. Haddad. Israel stared up. "What does Mesopotamia mean?" "The land between the rivers."

She pointed at a map on the wall. Two blue rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates, crossed a land that was mostly dry and brown. "Hardly any rain fell there in summer," said Ms. Haddad, "so people chose to close to the rivers. Water decided where the first towns grew."

"But the rivers were tricky," she went on. "Every spring, snow melted in the mountains, and the rivers flooded. Sometimes a flood washed away a whole village." "And yet farmers were happy after floods?" asked Sophia, reading a sign. "Yes, because the water left behind a layer of , soft, rich soil that was perfect for growing barley."

The farmers didn't just wait for the river. They dug long canals and ditches to carry water to their fields. This let them grow crops even in the hottest, driest months. "They changed their to survive in it," said Ms. Haddad.

With more water, farmers grew more barley than their families could eat. "That extra food was a ," said Ms. Haddad. "And when not everybody had to farm, some people could become potters, weavers, builders and priests." Towns grew into cities like Uruk, with tens of thousands of people.

Sophia looked at a clay tablet the size of a cookie. Tiny wedge-shaped marks covered it. "Is that writing?" "It's cuneiform, one of the first kinds of writing in the world," said Ms. Haddad. "Many early tablets are lists of barley, sheep and beer, because people needed to keep track of all that surplus." With cities, rulers, laws and writing, Mesopotamia became a .

"So the rivers only gave good things?" asked Israel. Ms. Haddad shook her head. "Over hundreds of years, irrigation water left salt behind in the soil. In some places, the land became too salty to grow wheat, so farmers switched to barley, which can handle more salt." "The river gave," said Sophia, "and the people's changes took some of it back."

On the bus back, Israel looked out at the Chicago River. "Did people settle here because of water too?" "Exactly," said Ms. Haddad. "And we changed our river too. Chicago once reversed its flow." Israel's eyes went wide. "Mesopotamia was cool, but that is wild."

Chapter 1: The painting made later

The Constitution and how to judge a historical source

Grade 5 · Social studies · SS.5.H.2 · SS.5.CV.2

A story about the Constitutional Convention of 1787 and about asking questions of a painting.

Key words

The story

Mr. Okafor turned on the screen. A big painting appeared: a room with tall windows, curtains and dozens of men in long coats. A tall man stood on a platform, holding a paper. "This is the Constitutional ," said Mr. Okafor. "Philadelphia, 1787. Here, the wrote the of the United States."

"Wow," said Daniel. "So this is what it looked like." Mr. Okafor smiled. "Are you sure? Before we decide, let's ask the painting some questions. Every has a , a date and a ."

The painting was made by an artist named Junius Brutus Stearns, in the year 1856. Daniel counted on his fingers. "From 1787 to 1856... that's 69 years later!" "Exactly," said Mr. Okafor. "Stearns was not in the room. He was not even born yet. So the painting shows what he imagined, not what he saw."

Athena looked closely. "Then it's not a photo. It's like a story in pictures." "Good thinking," said the teacher. "Some details may be true, and others may be the artist's ideas. Historians have counted 39 delegates in the painting, but on the day they signed, only 38 were in the room."

"Why would he paint it like that?" asked Selena. "Look at George Washington," said Mr. Okafor. "He stands higher than everyone, with light shining on him. Stearns wanted people in 1856 to see Washington as a great leader." The painting had a purpose: to make people feel proud.

"So is the painting useless?" asked Daniel. "Not at all," said the teacher. "It's very useful for learning what Americans in 1856 thought about the Convention. But to learn what really happened in 1787, we need sources from 1787, like the notes that James Madison wrote during the meetings."

Then Mr. Okafor asked a harder question. "Look at everyone in the painting. Who is missing?" The class looked again. Selena raised her hand. "There are no women," she said. "And there are no Black people and no Native Americans."

"That's true about the real Convention too," said Mr. Okafor. "Only white men were delegates. Women, enslaved people and Native nations had no voice in writing the Constitution, even though its rules would affect them." The class was quiet for a moment.

"Over many years, people fought to change those rules," the teacher added. "We'll learn about them this year." Athena drew the painting in her sketchbook. Then she added a few more people in the doorway: a woman, a Black man and a Native American girl. "My version," she said. "Painted in the 21st century, by Athena. Purpose: to show everyone."

Chapter 2: Three branches and an eagle

The three branches of government and how they have changed

Grade 5 · Social studies · SS.5.CV.1

A story about the three branches of government at the local, state and national levels.

Tres ramas y un águila. Un cuento sobre las tres ramas del gobierno en el nivel local, estatal y nacional.

Key words

The story

On a field trip downtown, Mr. Okafor stopped the class in Federal Plaza, next to a giant red sculpture. "Look at these buildings," he said. "Inside them, people work for the national government: judges, officials and members of Congress have offices here."

Elías unrolled a drawing he had made in class: a tree with three thick branches. "The government is like a tree," he said. "The makes the laws. The carries them out. And the decides what the laws mean."

"Why three?" asked Israel. "Wouldn't one boss be faster?" "Faster, maybe," said Mr. Okafor, "but dangerous. The people who wrote the Constitution didn't want a king. So they used : each branch can limit the others. Congress makes a law, the president can veto it, and the courts can decide if it follows the Constitution."

"And it's not only in Washington, D.C.," the teacher continued. "There are three . In Chicago, the mayor leads the city and the City Council makes city laws. In Springfield, the governor leads Illinois and the General Assembly makes state laws. And in Washington, D.C., there are the president and Congress."

Sophia noticed a round bronze on the building, with an eagle in the middle. "What's that bird holding?" she asked. "That's the Great Seal of the United States," said Mr. Okafor. "The eagle holds an olive branch in one claw and 13 arrows in the other."

"The olive branch stands for peace, and the arrows stand for being ready to defend the country," the teacher explained. "And look: the eagle's head is turned toward the olive branch. It means the country prefers peace." Sophia looked again. "So a picture can say something without words."

Back on the bus, Israel asked, "Has the government always been the same?" "No," said Mr. Okafor. "In 1789, the Supreme Court had six justices. Today it has nine. And the House of Representatives started with 65 members. Now it has 435, because the country grew."

"The jobs of the branches have changed too," he added. "Today the president leads a huge executive branch with millions of workers, from mail carriers to park rangers. In 1789, it was very small." Elías grabbed a pencil and added many little leaves to his tree. "It's still growing," he said.

At home that night, Israel's little cousin wanted to play. "I'm the president," she said, "and I make all the rules." Israel shook his head seriously. "Sorry. Congress makes the rules, you carry them out, and Grandma is the Supreme Court." Grandma, from the kitchen, agreed.

Salma's mother bought a notebook, a box of crayons and a bottle of juice. Salma had added up the prices in her head. But when the receipt came out, the total was higher. "Mom, the machine made a mistake!" she whispered.

The story

The class met at Union Station, in downtown Chicago. On one of the long wooden benches, Mr. Okafor unrolled an old map of the railroads in the 1880s. Dozens of lines crossed it, like the strings of a giant spider web. "Look," said Elías. "All the lines meet here, in Chicago."

"Why didn't every town make everything for itself?" asked Sophia. "Because it's better to ," said the teacher. "Farmers on the prairie were great at growing wheat. Factories in Chicago were great at making tools. With the railroad, they could with each other."

Daniel turned on his tablet. "Look what I found." It was an old photo from May 10, 1869. Two steam engines stood face to face in the desert of Utah, and a crowd of men cheered between them.

The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

Ms. Novak dropped a bundle of colored strings on Elías's desk. "The largest in the Americas kept its records with these," she said. "No paper, no alphabet. Just strings and knots." Elías held one up. "How can you run an empire with string?"

"In the 1400s and early 1500s, the Inca Empire stretched for thousands of miles along the Andes mountains of South America, with millions of people," said Ms. Novak. "To keep track of them, the Inca used the : a main cord with many strings hanging from it, each one tied with knots."

She showed them how the numbers worked. "The knots stand for numbers in base ten, like ours. A knot near the bottom of a string counts ones, higher up it counts tens, and higher still, hundreds. An empty space works like a zero." Daniel tied two knots in the hundreds place, none in the tens and three in the ones. "Two hundred three," said Athena.

"What did they count?" asked Daniel. "Almost everything," said Ms. Novak. "Khipus recorded the of each town, the corn and potatoes in the storehouses, and how many days of work each family owed the empire. Colors and the way strings were tied could add meaning too." Special officials, the khipu keepers, made the khipus and read them aloud to leaders.

"So we can read them now?" asked Elías. Ms. Novak shook her head. "We can read many of the numbers. But Spanish writers in the 1500s said that some khipus also held stories, laws and histories, and nobody today knows how to read those parts. When the Spanish conquered the Inca, the khipu keepers lost their jobs, and much of the knowledge was lost."

"Then how do historians know about the Inca?" asked Athena. "From many kinds of sources," said Ms. Novak. "Khipus are a made by the Inca themselves. But most written accounts come from Spanish conquerors and priests." "Those writers had a : many wanted to show that the conquest was right. So historians compare their accounts with khipus, buildings, objects and the memories of Andean communities today."

She told them that about a thousand khipus survive in museums around the world, and that researchers put their data into computer databases to look for patterns. In a few villages in the Andes, communities kept khipus as treasures into the 1900s. "The always has holes," she said. "A good historian notices what's missing, not just what's there."

At the end of class, Elías made his own khipu. He tied one string for each person in his family and used a red string for the number of birthdays in December. "Can you read it?" he asked Athena. She counted the knots. "Five people, two December birthdays." "See?" said Elías. "You don't need letters to keep a record."

Check your thinking

The knots closest to the main cord count the biggest place values, like hundreds, and the knots near the bottom count ones. A khipu was made with skill: the colors, the spacing and the way the cords were spun and tied all had to be careful and exact. Many objects, like quilts, pottery and baskets, are both useful and works of art.

Glossary

empire
A large area with many peoples and lands, ruled by one government.
imperio: Un territorio grande con muchos pueblos y tierras, gobernado por un solo gobierno.
khipu
A record made of knotted cotton or wool strings, used by the Inca to store numbers and information.
quipu: Un registro hecho con cordones anudados de algodón o lana, que usaban los incas para guardar números e información.
census
An official count of the people who live in a place.
censo: Un recuento oficial de las personas que viven en un lugar.
primary source
A source made by people who lived at the time, like a letter, a photo or a khipu.
fuente primaria: Una fuente hecha por personas que vivieron en esa época, como una carta, una foto o un quipu.
bias
A one-sided view that can make a source unfair or incomplete.
sesgo: Una visión parcial que puede hacer que una fuente sea injusta o incompleta.
historical record
All the sources that survive from the past and help us learn what happened.
registro histórico: Todas las fuentes que se conservan del pasado y nos ayudan a saber qué pasó.

  1. AQuestion A. How did the Inca keep records of their empire?

    ¿Cómo llevaban los incas los registros de su imperio?

    The Inca used the khipu: a main cord with many strings hanging from it, each tied with knots.

  2. BQuestion B. How did the knots show numbers?

    ¿Cómo mostraban los nudos los números?

    Knots near the bottom count ones, higher up tens, and higher still hundreds; an empty space works like a zero.

  3. CQuestion C. What did khipus record?

    ¿Qué registraban los quipus?

    Khipus recorded the census, the food in storehouses and how many days of work each family owed.

  4. DQuestion D. Why can't anyone read every part of the khipus today?

    ¿Por qué hoy nadie puede leer todas las partes de los quipus?

    After the Spanish conquest, the khipu keepers lost their jobs, and much of the knowledge to read them was lost.

  5. EQuestion E. Why must historians be careful with Spanish accounts of the Inca?

    ¿Por qué deben los historiadores tener cuidado con los relatos españoles sobre los incas?

    Many Spanish writers wanted to show that the conquest was right, so historians compare their accounts with other sources.

Analyze the sources

A historian wants to know how many people lived in an Inca town. She has a khipu from the town and a report written by a Spanish official in 1570. List one strength and one limitation of each source, and explain how using both is better than using only one.

Analiza las fuentes. Una historiadora quiere saber cuántas personas vivían en un pueblo inca. Tiene un quipu del pueblo y un informe escrito por un funcionario español en 1570. Escribe una fortaleza y una limitación de cada fuente, y explica por qué usar las dos es mejor que usar una sola.

Answer in your notebook or talk it over with a classmate. Use evidence from the story.

Responde en tu cuaderno o coméntalo con un compañero. Usa evidencia del cuento.

Check your thinking

Khipu. Strength: it was made by the Inca themselves at the time. Limitation: we can read the numbers, but we may not know exactly what each string counted. Spanish report. Strength: it is written in words we can read. Limitation: it was written after the conquest and could be biased, for example to raise taxes. Using both lets her compare the numbers and notice if one source seems wrong or one-sided.

Go further

Think about the records your school keeps, like attendance or grades. What would a historian in 500 years learn from them, and what would be missing?

Para ir más lejos. Piensa en los registros que guarda tu escuela, como la asistencia o las calificaciones. ¿Qué aprendería de ellos un historiador dentro de 500 años y qué faltaría?

Check your thinking

A historian could learn how many students came to school, which classes they took and how they did. Missing: how students felt, what they talked about, their friendships and their families' stories. Like khipus, records show numbers well but often leave out people's voices.

The story

The class bus drove almost all the way across Illinois to a spot near Collinsville, not far from the Mississippi River. In front of them rose a huge hill with flat sides, covered in grass. "That's no ordinary hill," said the park guide. "People built it."

"About a thousand years ago, this was Cahokia, the largest city north of Mexico," the guide explained. "Thousands of people lived here, maybe more than ten thousand." Today the people who built it are called Mississippians.

The people of Cahokia built more than one hundred . They carried the earth in baskets, load after load, for many years. The biggest one, Monks Mound, is about 100 feet tall, as high as a ten-story building. Salma counted the steps on the way up, but halfway there she lost count and decided to count clouds instead.

From the top they could see a big green plaza. "People gathered here for ceremonies and games," said the guide. "All around were houses and fields, where they would corn, squash and sunflowers." Athena sketched nonstop in her notebook.

In the museum there were glass cases with arrowheads, pots and seashells. "Seashells? This far from the ocean?" Israel asked. "They're , objects that people made or used long ago," the guide explained. "The shells came from the Gulf of Mexico, and the copper came from the Great Lakes. That tells us Cahokia carried on with faraway peoples."

Over time, the city emptied out, and its people moved to other places. Centuries later, the peoples of the Illinois Confederation, such as the Kaskaskia, the Peoria and the Cahokia, lived in the area. The site is named for the Cahokia, and our state is named Illinois after these .

In the last room, a sign explained that these peoples are not just part of history. The guide told them that today the Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, a Native , includes descendants of the Illinois peoples, and that thousands of Native people from many nations live in Chicago. "They're still here," said Salma, "with their families, their languages and their traditions."

On the bus, Athena showed her drawing: the plaza, the mound and, at the very top, a girl counting clouds. "That's me!" Salma laughed.

Glossary

mound
A small hill that people made by piling up earth.
farm
To plant and care for crops, such as corn, to grow food.
artifact
An object made or used by people long ago, like a pot or an arrowhead.
trade
Buying, selling or exchanging things with other people or other nations.
archaeologist
A scientist who studies how people lived long ago by looking at what they left behind.
Native peoples
Peoples who lived on a land long before Europeans arrived and who still live today.
nation
A people with its own history, culture and government. Many Native peoples are nations.

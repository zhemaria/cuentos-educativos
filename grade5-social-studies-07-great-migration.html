The story

El cuento está en inglés. Pulsa «Listen to the story» para escucharlo y toca las palabras verdes cuando no entiendas una.

On Sunday afternoon, Daniel and Israel visited Daniel's great-grandmother in Bronzeville. She opened an old photo album. Inside, between two pictures, there was a faded paper train ticket. "My mother bought this ticket in Mississippi," she said. "It brought our family to Chicago."

"Why did she leave?" asked Israel. "In the South, there were unfair laws called ," said great-grandma. "Black people couldn't go to the same schools, eat in the same restaurants or vote freely. The jobs paid very little, and many families lived in fear."

"So she came north looking for a better life," said Daniel. "Like millions of others," said his great-grandmother. "From about 1916 to 1970, about six million African Americans moved from the South to cities in the North and West. It's called the ."

"How did they know about Chicago?" asked Israel. "From a called the Chicago Defender," she said. "It was a Black newspaper, started in 1905. It told people about jobs in Chicago's factories, and Pullman porters, who worked on the trains, carried copies to the South."

The next day, Daniel shared the story in class. Mr. Okafor showed a chart. "In 1910, about 44,000 Black people lived in Chicago. By 1930, there were more than 230,000." "The city changed," he said. "New churches, businesses, newspapers and music filled neighborhoods like Bronzeville."

"Music?" asked Israel. "The people who came from the South brought their music with them," said the teacher. "The was born in the South, among African Americans. In Chicago, blues musicians started playing louder, with electric guitars, in crowded clubs."

"And a musician named Thomas A. Dorsey, who came to Chicago from Georgia, mixed the blues with church music. He is called the father of music." He worked at Pilgrim Baptist Church, in Bronzeville. "A young singer from New Orleans named Mahalia Jackson moved to Chicago too, and she became one of the most famous gospel singers in the world."

"So the Great Migration didn't only change where people lived," said Daniel. "It changed the music of the whole country." "Exactly," said Mr. Okafor. "And the music tells us about the people who made it: their hard times, their faith and their hope."

That evening, Daniel called his great-grandmother. "Grandma, can you sing me a gospel song?" She laughed and sang a few lines over the phone, strong and slow. When she finished, Daniel said, "Mr. Okafor says that's history." "Baby," she said, "that's just Sunday."

Check your thinking

The poem has rhyming lines (tune and croon, floor) and words that repeat like a beat, so it sounds like a song. Music about hard times helps people feel that they are not alone, and singing about a problem can give people strength. Poems and songs connect to the history and feelings of the people who made them.

Glossary

migration
When many people move from one place to another to live.
migración: Cuando muchas personas se mudan de un lugar a otro para vivir.
Great Migration
The move of about six million African Americans from the South to cities in the North and West, from about 1916 to 1970.
Gran Migración: El traslado de unos seis millones de afroamericanos del Sur a ciudades del Norte y del Oeste, entre 1916 y 1970 aproximadamente.
segregation
Unfair laws and customs that kept Black people and white people apart.
segregación: Leyes y costumbres injustas que separaban a las personas negras de las blancas.
newspaper
Printed pages with news that come out every day or every week.
periódico: Páginas impresas con noticias que salen cada día o cada semana.
blues
A kind of music created by African Americans in the South, often about hard times, sung with deep feeling.
blues: Un tipo de música creado por afroamericanos en el Sur, muchas veces sobre tiempos difíciles, cantado con mucho sentimiento.
gospel
Religious music with strong voices and joyful rhythms, which grew in Black churches.
góspel: Música religiosa con voces fuertes y ritmos alegres, que creció en las iglesias afroamericanas.

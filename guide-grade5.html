1. Tips for using the stories

These are the small things that make the biggest difference, and the ones that are easiest to forget on a busy day. They work for both books.

Before reading: give the story a purpose

  • Look at the picture together for two minutes before anyone reads. Ask: What do you see? What do you think? What do you wonder?
  • Teach two or three of the science or social studies words first. Each story lists them at the top, and the glossary opens with one tap.
  • Write one question on the board that the story will answer. Students read to find the answer, not just to finish.

Antes de leer: mirar la ilustración dos minutos (¿qué ves?, ¿qué piensas?, ¿qué te preguntas?), enseñar dos o tres palabras clave y escribir en la pizarra una pregunta que el cuento va a contestar.

During reading: slow down

  • Read the story aloud first, or let Lumi read it with the voice button. Then students read it again on their own or with a partner. A second reading is where comprehension happens.
  • Stop at the pause to connect and at the questions. They are not interruptions: they are the lesson.
  • One story per session is enough (20 to 30 minutes). Do not assign all twelve at once; follow the order of your class units.

Durante la lectura: leer primero en voz alta (o con la voz de Lumi) y luego que lo relean solos o en pareja; detenerse en las pausas y preguntas; un cuento por sesión.

Questions: wait, then listen

  • After you ask a question, count silently to five before calling on anyone. Most hands go up after the third second.
  • Use turn and talk: every student tells a partner first, then a few share with the class. Everybody thinks, not only the fastest.
  • Ask students to choose an answer before they tap it. The button confirms what they already said.

Preguntas: esperar cinco segundos antes de dar la palabra; que todos lo digan primero a un compañero; que elijan la respuesta antes de tocarla.

«Ayuda en español»: a bridge, not a replacement

  • The button translates instructions, questions, the glossary and the challenge. The story itself stays in English on purpose.
  • Suggested routine: first reading in English, then turn the help on to check understanding, then a final reading without it.
  • Turn the help off little by little during the year. Celebrate the day a student no longer needs it.
  • Spanish at home is an asset. Invite families to talk about the story in the language they speak best.

La «Ayuda en español» es un puente, no un sustituto: primera lectura en inglés, luego la ayuda para comprobar, y una última lectura sin ella. Retirarla poco a poco durante el año. El español en casa es una fortaleza.

Universal Design for Learning: let every student in

  • Show students the reading supports on the first day: bigger or smaller text (A+ / A−), high contrast, the voice and printing.
  • Accept more than one way to answer the challenge: writing, drawing a model, explaining out loud or recording a short audio.
  • Give sentence starters for the challenge: I think… because… The evidence is…
  • For students without a device, print the story with the «Print or save as PDF» button.

Diseño Universal para el Aprendizaje: enseñar los apoyos el primer día (A+/A−, alto contraste, voz, imprimir); aceptar varias formas de contestar el reto (escribir, dibujar, decirlo en voz alta); dar inicios de oración; imprimir el cuento para quien no tiene tableta.

The challenge: claim, evidence, reasoning

  • Every story ends with «Challenge: explain your thinking». Ask for three parts: a claim (what I think), evidence (what I saw or read) and reasoning (why the evidence supports the claim).
  • Use the challenge as an exit ticket. Two or three good sentences are worth more than a long paragraph.
  • Keep the answers in a folder. In May, students compare their first and last answers and see how much they grew.

El reto: pedir afirmación, evidencia y razonamiento. Usarlo como boleto de salida y guardar las respuestas en una carpeta para comparar al final del año.

Welcome wrong ideas

  • The stories often show a character with a wrong idea (a cousin, a friend). That is on purpose: wrong ideas are the starting point of science and history.
  • Before the reading, ask students what they think. Write their ideas on the board without correcting them, and come back to the list after the story.
  • Each activity below has a watch for note with the most common misunderstanding.

Recibir las ideas equivocadas: anotar lo que piensan antes de leer, sin corregir, y volver a la lista después del cuento. Cada actividad trae una nota con la confusión más común.

Hands-on activities: safety first

  • Wear safety goggles for anything that can splash. The adult handles hot water, heat, flames and sharp tools.
  • Check food allergies before using food (sugar, flour, gumdrops, bread). Scientists never taste the materials, even when they are food.
  • Keep decomposition bags and bottles sealed and throw them away closed.
  • Every activity can also be done as a teacher demonstration or with a video if materials or time are short.

Seguridad: gafas cuando algo pueda salpicar; el adulto maneja el agua caliente, el calor y lo filoso; revisar alergias antes de usar alimentos; nunca probar los materiales; las bolsas de descomposición se botan cerradas.

Beyond the classroom

  • Share the address www.lumi-educa.com with families. The stories work on a phone, a tablet or a computer, and the voice can read them aloud.
  • Story 12 reviews the whole year. Use it in May as a celebration and as a review before the final assessment.
  • Revisit favorite stories. Children love rereading, and every new reading finds something new.

Fuera del salón: compartir www.lumi-educa.com con las familias; usar el cuento 12 como repaso y celebración de fin de año; releer los cuentos favoritos.

2. Science

Each story has a hands-on activity with classroom materials, questions to talk about and a note on the most common misunderstanding. The activities are optional and can be done before the story (to wonder) or after it (to check the idea).

Cada cuento de ciencias tiene una actividad práctica con materiales del salón, preguntas para conversar y una nota sobre la confusión más común. Se pueden hacer antes del cuento (para despertar preguntas) o después (para comprobar la idea).

1

The popcorn mystery

Matter is made of particles too small to see · 5-PS1-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Where did the sugar go?

Materials: clear cups, warm water, sugar, a kitchen scale, a cotton ball, vanilla extract.

  1. Weigh a cup of water and a spoonful of sugar together. Stir the sugar in until it disappears, and weigh again: the weight does not change.
  2. Put a few drops of vanilla on a cotton ball in one corner of the room. Students raise a hand when the smell reaches them, as in the story.
  3. Students draw a model of the sugar water with dots for particles, before and after stirring.

Discussion questions

If we cannot see the sugar anymore, how do we know it is still there? How did the smell travel across the room?

Watch for: Many students think that dissolved sugar is gone. The scale is the evidence that it is still there, in particles too small to see.

Safety: No tasting, even though it is sugar.

Actividad: ¿Adónde se fue el azúcar? Se pesa el agua con el azúcar antes y después de disolverlo (pesa lo mismo), se pone vainilla en una esquina para ver cómo viaja el olor y se dibuja un modelo de partículas.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-PS1-1. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

2

The bag that puffed up

New substances and weight that stays the same · 5-PS1-2 · 5-PS1-4

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Weigh the reaction

Materials: zip-top bags, baking soda, vinegar, a small cup, a digital scale, safety goggles.

  1. Put a spoonful of baking soda in the bag and vinegar in the small cup, standing inside the bag. Seal the bag and weigh everything.
  2. Tip the cup inside the closed bag. Observe: bubbles, cold, the bag puffs up. Weigh again.
  3. Open the bag, let the gas out and weigh one last time. Compare the three numbers.

Discussion questions

Why did the weight stay the same while the bag was closed? Why did it go down when we opened it? What was the new substance?

Watch for: Students often think gases weigh nothing. The third weighing shows that the gas that escaped had weight.

Safety: Goggles on. Do not overfill the bag: it can pop.

Actividad: Pesar la reacción. Bicarbonato y vinagre dentro de una bolsa cerrada sobre la balanza: el peso no cambia; al abrirla y dejar salir el gas, el peso baja, porque el gas también pesa.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-PS1-2 · 5-PS1-4. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

3

The case of the white powders

Identifying materials by their properties · 5-PS1-3

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Mystery powders lab

Materials: salt, sugar, flour and baking soda in cups labeled A to D, magnifying glasses, water, vinegar, droppers, black paper.

  1. Groups observe each powder on black paper with a magnifying glass and record the shape of the grains.
  2. They test each powder with a few drops of water and then with a few drops of vinegar, and record what happens in a table.
  3. With the table, they identify each cup and explain which property gave the decisive clue.

Discussion questions

Which test was the most useful? Why is one property not enough to identify a material?

Watch for: Students tend to decide by looks alone ("it's white, so it's salt"). Insist on at least two tests before a conclusion.

Safety: No tasting: scientists never taste unknown substances, even in the kitchen.

Actividad: Laboratorio de polvos misteriosos. Sal, azúcar, harina y bicarbonato sin etiqueta: se observan con lupa y se prueban con agua y con vinagre hasta identificar cada uno con al menos dos pruebas. No se prueban con la boca.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-PS1-3. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

4

Why the ball always comes down

Gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth · 5-PS2-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Down is toward the center

Materials: a globe or a large ball, small paper figures, tape, a string with a washer tied to the end.

  1. Tape paper people on the globe in Chicago, Australia, Brazil and the North Pole. Students draw an arrow from each person's feet that shows "down".
  2. Hold the string with the washer still: it always hangs straight down. Explain that it points toward the center of Earth.
  3. Students drop a book and a crumpled sheet of paper from the same height at the same time and observe.

Discussion questions

Why don't the people in Australia fall off? If every arrow points down, where do all the arrows meet?

Watch for: Many children picture "down" as the bottom of the page or the bottom of the globe. The arrows should all point to the center of Earth.

Actividad: «Abajo» es hacia el centro. Se pegan figuras en un globo terráqueo en distintos países y se dibuja hacia dónde es «abajo» para cada una: todas las flechas apuntan al centro de la Tierra.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-PS2-1. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

5

The sunshine taco

The energy in food was once energy from the sun · 5-PS3-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Trace the energy of your lunch

Materials: cards with drawings (sun, corn, chicken, tortilla, child), yarn or arrows, paper.

  1. Groups put the cards in order from the sun to the child and join them with arrows that show where the energy goes.
  2. Each student draws the path of the energy in their own breakfast or lunch, as in the challenge.
  3. Share: is there any food whose energy did not come from the sun?

Discussion questions

Where was the energy in your taco before it was in the chicken? And before that?

Watch for: Students often think plants get their energy from the soil or that food "makes" energy. The energy comes from the sun and is stored by the plant.

Actividad: Seguir la energía del almuerzo. Con tarjetas (sol, maíz, pollo, tortilla, niño) se arma la cadena con flechas, y cada estudiante dibuja de dónde vino la energía de su propio desayuno.

6

The tree made of air

Plants get the materials they need mainly from air and water · 5-LS1-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: A small van Helmont experiment

Materials: pots, potting soil, bean seeds, a kitchen scale, water, a notebook.

  1. Weigh the dry soil of each pot and write the number on the pot.
  2. Plant the beans, water them and let them grow for three or four weeks. Record the height of the plants each week.
  3. Take out the plants, let the soil dry and weigh it again. Compare: the plant grew a lot, and the soil almost did not change.

Discussion questions

If the plant did not take its matter from the soil, where did it come from? What did van Helmont discover?

Watch for: The most common idea is that plants "eat" soil. The experiment shows that most of the matter of a plant comes from the air (carbon dioxide) and the water.

Actividad: Un pequeño experimento de van Helmont. Se pesa la tierra, se siembran frijoles y, tras tres o cuatro semanas, se vuelve a pesar: la planta creció mucho y la tierra casi no cambió. La materia viene sobre todo del aire y del agua.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-LS1-1. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

7

What the compost ate

Matter moves among plants, animals, decomposers and the environment · 5-LS2-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Decomposition bags and a yarn food web

Materials: clear zip-top bags, a slice of bread, a piece of apple, leaves, a few drops of water, tape, a ball of yarn.

  1. Put each material in its own bag with a few drops of water, seal it with tape and observe it every two or three days for three weeks. Draw the changes.
  2. In a circle, each student plays a living thing of a park (grass, tree, squirrel, robin, worm, mushroom). Pass the yarn from "who is eaten" to "who eats".
  3. Ask: what happens to the web if the decomposers disappear?

Discussion questions

Where does the matter of a fallen leaf go? Why do decomposers matter in an ecosystem?

Watch for: Students often leave decomposers out of the web or think that matter "disappears" when something rots. It turns into soil and air again.

Safety: Never open the decomposition bags: mold can cause allergies. Throw them away sealed.

Actividad: Bolsas de descomposición y red alimentaria con estambre. Pan, manzana y hojas en bolsas cerradas para observar durante tres semanas (nunca se abren), y una red alimentaria en círculo con estambre.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-LS2-1. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

8

The star next door

The sun looks brighter than other stars because it is closer · 5-ESS1-1

Open the story

Hands-on activity: The flashlight test

Materials: two identical flashlights, a dark hallway or room, a tape measure.

  1. One student holds a flashlight close to the class and another holds the same kind of flashlight far away, at the end of the hallway.
  2. The class compares how bright they look. Then the far flashlight comes closer, step by step.
  3. Students write a sentence that connects the test with the sun and the other stars.

Discussion questions

The two flashlights are the same. Why does one look brighter? What does this tell us about the sun?

Watch for: Many students believe the sun is the biggest and brightest star of all. It only looks that way because it is much closer to us than any other star.

Safety: Never look directly at the sun or shine a flashlight into someone's eyes.

Actividad: La prueba de las linternas. Dos linternas iguales, una cerca y otra lejos: la cercana parece más brillante. Así pasa con el Sol, que parece la estrella más brillante porque está más cerca.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-ESS1-1. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

9

Three shadows in one day

Patterns of shadows, day and night, and stars across the seasons · 5-ESS1-2

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Shadow tracking and a graph

Materials: sidewalk chalk, a tape measure, a sunny day, graph paper, a flashlight and a globe.

  1. Like the class in the story, trace the shadow of the same student on the same spot at three times of day. Measure each shadow.
  2. Make a bar graph with the lengths. Describe the pattern: long, short, long.
  3. In a dark room, turn the globe in front of the flashlight to show day and night.

Discussion questions

When was the shadow shortest? Why? What would happen to the shadows in December?

Watch for: Students may think the sun moves around Earth. Remind them that the sun only seems to move because Earth turns.

Safety: Never look directly at the sun.

Actividad: Seguir la sombra y hacer una gráfica. Se traza con tiza la sombra de un estudiante a tres horas del día, se mide y se grafica; luego, con una linterna y un globo terráqueo, se muestra el día y la noche.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-ESS1-2. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

10

The lake that touches everything

Earth's four spheres and where water is found · 5-ESS2-1 · 5-ESS2-2

Open the story

Hands-on activity: How much water can we drink?

Materials: a 1-liter bottle of water, a measuring cup, a small cup, a dropper, four signs (geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, biosphere).

  1. The liter of water stands for all the water on Earth. Pour out 30 mL into a small cup: that is all the fresh water. The rest is salt water.
  2. From the 30 mL, most is frozen in glaciers or underground. Take out a single drop: that is roughly the fresh water in lakes and rivers.
  3. With the four signs, students act out a rainy day in the neighborhood and say which spheres are interacting.

Discussion questions

Why is it so important to protect Lake Michigan? Give an example of two spheres interacting on a windy day.

Watch for: Students usually think there is a lot of drinkable water because Earth looks blue. Almost all of it is salty or frozen.

Actividad: ¿Cuánta agua podemos beber? Un litro representa toda el agua de la Tierra: solo unos 30 mL son agua dulce, y de ellos apenas una gota está en lagos y ríos. Después se representan las cuatro esferas en un día de lluvia.

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 5-ESS2-1 · 5-ESS2-2. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

11

The trash catcher

Communities protect Earth's resources and environment · 3-5-ETS1-1 · 3-5-ETS1-2 · 3-5-ETS1-3

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Design a river trash catcher

Materials: a long plastic tub with water, straws, mesh or netting, craft sticks, tape, bits of paper and plastic for trash, small pebbles for "fish".

  1. Present the problem and agree on the criteria (catch the floating trash, let the water and the "fish" pass) and the constraints (materials, time).
  2. Groups draw a design, build a prototype and test it by making a slow current in the tub.
  3. They count how much trash was caught, improve the design and test again.

Discussion questions

What changed between your first and your second prototype? What is the difference between a criterion and a constraint?

Watch for: Students expect the first design to work. Testing, failing and improving is exactly what engineers do.

Safety: Wipe up spilled water right away so nobody slips.

Actividad: Diseñar un atrapabasura para el río. En una bandeja con agua, los grupos diseñan, construyen, prueban y mejoran un prototipo que atrape la basura flotante y deje pasar el agua y los «peces».

Illinois Learning Standards for Science (NGSS): 3-5-ETS1-1 · 3-5-ETS1-2 · 3-5-ETS1-3. The challenge at the end of the story is the evidence of learning.

12

The science fair

Review of the whole year · 5-PS1 · 5-PS2 · 5-PS3 · 5-LS1 · 5-LS2 · 5-ESS1 · 5-ESS2 · 5-ESS3

Open the story

Hands-on activity: Our own science fair

Materials: the materials of the year's activities, poster paper, stamp cards.

  1. Each group chooses one idea from the year and prepares a small station that shows it with an object or a short demonstration.
  2. Classmates or families visit the stations with a stamp card, as in the story.
  3. Each visitor writes, for two stations, what was shown and what evidence supports the idea.

Discussion questions

Which idea of the year surprised you most? What evidence changed your mind?

Watch for: Stations can turn into a show. Ask every group to say clearly what the evidence is.

Safety: Review the safety rules of each activity that is repeated.

Actividad: Nuestra propia feria de ciencias. Cada grupo prepara una estación con una idea del año; los visitantes sellan una tarjeta y escriben qué se mostró y qué evidencia lo apoya.

3. Social studies

Each story has a pause to connect that a child can do alone on a tablet: look at a picture from the time, read verses from a public domain song or poem, or listen to a rhythm. The activities in this guide are for the whole class or small groups. They are optional.

The arts connections follow Anchor Standard 11 (Connecting) of the Illinois Arts Learning Standards. The stories use only well-established historical facts and do not put invented words in the mouths of real people. When students write from the point of view of a historical person, present it clearly as their own creative writing.

Cada cuento de estudios sociales tiene una pausa para conectar con las artes (una obra, un poema o una canción de la época) y aquí una actividad de grupo, preguntas para conversar y el estándar de Illinois.

1

The painting made later

The Constitution and how to judge a historical source · SS.5.H.2 · SS.5.CV.2

Open the story

Group activity: Living picture (tableau)

TH:Cn11.2.5

In groups, students recreate the Stearns painting as a frozen picture. When the teacher taps a student on the shoulder, that student says one sentence about what their delegate is thinking. Then ask: who is missing from this picture? Add those people to the tableau and discuss how the meaning of the picture changes.

Discussion questions

Who made this source, when and why? What can a painting made in 1856 tell us, and what can it not tell us?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the painting by Junius Brutus Stearns. Take your time and look at every corner.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.5.H.2: use information about a historical source, including the creator, date, place of origin, intended audience and purpose, to judge how useful it is for studying a topic; and SS.5.CV.2: examine the origins and purposes of rules, laws and key provisions of the U.S. Constitution, and how they affected different groups of people. The challenge lets students show SS.5.H.2. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.5: recognize that people use art to inform or change beliefs, values or behaviors.

The road to freedom

The Underground Railroad in Illinois · SS.5.H.1

Open the story

Group activity: Call and response

MU:Cn11.1.5

Explain that call and response is a musical tradition with deep roots in African and African American music, heard in spirituals, work songs, gospel and the blues. Discussion questions

What risks did freedom seekers and their helpers take? Why were songs important to people who were enslaved?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Read these lines from the spiritual “Go Down, Moses” slowly, as if you were singing them.

4. The stories in one page · Grade 5

Before

  • Look at the picture for 2 minutes: see, think, wonder.
  • Teach 2 or 3 key words.
  • Write the question the story will answer.

During

  • Read aloud first; then students reread.
  • Stop at the pause and the questions.
  • Count to 5 before calling on anyone. Turn and talk.

After

  • Challenge: claim, evidence, reasoning.
  • Accept drawings and oral answers.
  • Keep the answer as an exit ticket.

Always

  • «Ayuda en español» after the first English reading; withdraw it little by little.
  • Show the supports: A+/A−, high contrast, voice, print.
  • Goggles, no tasting, the adult handles heat.

Antes: ilustración 2 minutos, 2 o 3 palabras clave, la pregunta del cuento. Durante: leer en voz alta y releer, detenerse en las pausas, esperar 5 segundos. Después: afirmación, evidencia y razonamiento; aceptar dibujos y respuestas orales. Siempre: la ayuda en español después de la primera lectura en inglés; mostrar los apoyos; seguridad.

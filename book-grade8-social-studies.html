Chapter 1: The almanac that wrote to Jefferson

Reading a primary source: Benjamin Banneker, the survey of the capital and his letter to Jefferson

Grade 8 · Social studies · SS.6-8.H.3.MdC

A story about a self-taught astronomer who helped mark the borders of the nation's capital, and a letter that asked the author of the Declaration of Independence a hard question.

The story

Ms. Novak unrolled a copy of an old map with straight streets and wide diagonal avenues. "This is a plan of the city of Washington from 1792," she said. "But before anyone could draw the streets, someone had to measure where the federal district began and ended. One of the people who helped was Benjamin Banneker."

"Banneker was a free African American born in Maryland in 1731," she said. "He spent most of his life on his family's farm, and he taught himself mathematics and astronomy with borrowed books and instruments." "Self-taught?" said Daniel. "Completely," said Ms. Novak.

"In 1791, Major Andrew Ellicott was hired to survey the borders of the new federal district, a square ten miles on each side," she said. "He chose Banneker as an assistant. At night, Banneker used the stars to help find exact positions, because a needed the sky to know where he stood on the Earth."

After the survey, Banneker finished his own , with the positions of the Sun, the Moon and the planets for a whole year, all calculated by hand. "On August 19, 1791, he sent a copy to Thomas Jefferson, the Secretary of State and the author of the Declaration of Independence," said Ms. Novak. "With it, he wrote a long letter. He reminded Jefferson that the Declaration said that all men are created equal, and he pointed out the of holding people in slavery."

"What did Jefferson say?" asked Salma. "He answered on August 30, politely, and thanked Banneker for the almanac," said Ms. Novak. "The next year, both letters were printed together in Philadelphia, so many more people could read them."

Then she gave each pair a copy of Banneker's letter, a , and three questions to write on the top: Who wrote it? Why? For whom? "The author is Banneker, a free Black scientist," said Selena. "His was to persuade Jefferson that Black people were equal in ability, and his almanac was the proof. His first was Jefferson, but when the letters were printed, the audience became the whole country."

"So asking who, why and for whom changes how you read it," said Israel. "Exactly," said Ms. Novak. "Every source has a point of view. Knowing it doesn't make the source less valuable. It helps you understand it."

Before the bell, Elías looked again at the map of Washington. "The capital was measured with the help of a man who couldn't vote and who had to remind the government what it had promised," he said. Sophia wrote in her notebook: A letter can be a calculation, too. Banneker proved his point with numbers.

The story

Athena had flown to visit her grandmother over the weekend. "From the airplane window, Illinois looked like a checkerboard," she said. "Perfect squares of fields, with roads running straight north and straight east." Ms. Novak smiled and projected a photo that looked just like what Athena described. "That checkerboard was drawn by a law," she said.

"On May 20, 1785, Congress passed the Land ," said Ms. Novak. "It said that the western lands would be measured before they were sold, in squares called , six miles on each side." Athena drew a big square on the board.

"Each township was divided into 36 , each one mile on each side," said Ms. Novak. "One section is 640 ." Athena divided her square into six rows of six small squares and numbered them. "So a quarter of a section is 160 acres," said Israel, who had his calculator out. "That's the size of a lot of family farms in the old days," said Ms. Novak.

"Here is my favorite part," said Ms. Novak. "The law reserved section number 16 in every township for the support of public schools." Athena circled square 16. "So schools were part of the plan from the start," said Salma.

Ms. Novak showed a map of Chicago. "Chicago's of streets fits into the pattern of townships and sections that began with this law," she said. "Many of our main streets are one mile apart, along the old section lines." Daniel counted on the map. "Madison to Roosevelt, Roosevelt to Cermak… they're all about a mile apart!"

Then Ms. Novak asked a harder question. "Before anyone drew these squares, who lived on this land?" "The Native nations," said Selena. "Yes," said Ms. Novak. "The land could be surveyed and sold only after Native nations gave it up in treaties, called , often under pressure from the government. The straight lines on the map didn't follow their villages, their rivers or their hunting grounds."

"So the same squares mean different things to different people," said Elías. "For a farmer from the East, a chance to own land. For a Native nation, a home divided on paper." "That's why we look at a map from more than one perspective," said Ms. Novak.

That afternoon, Athena found her school on the map. It wasn't in section 16, but it was close. Sophia wrote in her notebook: The land remembers the law. You can see it from the sky.

Chapter 4: Who counted, and who was counted

Rights and power: the first census of 1790 and the Three-Fifths Compromise

The story

"Every ten years, the government counts everyone who lives in the United States," said Ms. Novak. "It's called the , and the Constitution requires it. The first one began on August 2, 1790." She held up a drawing of a man on horseback with a notebook.

"There were no census offices yet," she said. "The count was done by U.S. marshals and their assistants, who rode from farm to farm and town to town. When they finished, they had counted 3,929,214 people." "Almost four million," said Israel. "Today Chicago and its suburbs have more than that."

"Why count everyone?" asked Selena. "Because the census decides ," said Ms. Novak. "The number of seats each state gets in the House of Representatives depends on its population. That's called . More people means more seats, and more power in Congress."

"So who was counted?" asked Ms. Novak. She wrote a phrase from the Constitution on the board: three fifths of all other persons. "The 'other persons' were people," she said. "In the Three-Fifths , each enslaved person counted as three fifths of a person when deciding a state's seats."

Daniel frowned. "But enslaved people couldn't vote." "Exactly," said Ms. Novak. "They had no rights and no voice, but counting them, even as a fraction, gave more seats and more power to the states that held them in slavery. The fraction was a deal about power, not about fairness." The class was quiet.

"Was anyone left out completely?" asked Salma. "Yes," said Ms. Novak. "The Constitution said 'Indians not taxed' were not counted. Most Native people belonged to their own nations and were left out of the count."

"The Three-Fifths rule ended after the Civil War," said Ms. Novak. "The Fourteenth , ratified in 1868, said that seats would be divided by counting the whole number of persons in each state." "So the count changed when the rights changed," said Elías.

"And the census still matters," said Ms. Novak. "It decides seats in Congress and how money for schools, roads and hospitals is shared." Sophia wrote in her notebook: A count is never just numbers. Ask who counted, who was counted, and why.

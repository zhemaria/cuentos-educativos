Connecting history and the arts

How to use this guide

Each story has a pause to connect that a child can do alone on a tablet: look at a picture from the time, read verses from a public domain song or poem, or listen to a rhythm. The activities in this guide are for the whole class or small groups. They are optional.

The arts connections follow Anchor Standard 11 (Connecting) of the Illinois Arts Learning Standards. The stories use only well-established historical facts and do not put invented words in the mouths of real people. When students write from the point of view of a historical person, present it clearly as their own creative writing.

Activities by story

1

The painting made later

The Constitution and how to judge a historical source · SS.5.H.2 · SS.5.CV.2

Open the story

Group activity: Living picture (tableau)

TH:Cn11.2.5

In groups, students recreate the Stearns painting as a frozen picture. When the teacher taps a student on the shoulder, that student says one sentence about what their delegate is thinking. Then ask: who is missing from this picture? Add those people to the tableau and discuss how the meaning of the picture changes.

Discussion questions

Who made this source, when and why? What can a painting made in 1856 tell us, and what can it not tell us?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the painting by Junius Brutus Stearns. Take your time and look at every corner.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.5.H.2: use information about a historical source, including the creator, date, place of origin, intended audience and purpose, to judge how useful it is for studying a topic; and SS.5.CV.2: examine the origins and purposes of rules, laws and key provisions of the U.S. Constitution, and how they affected different groups of people. The challenge lets students show SS.5.H.2. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.5: recognize that people use art to inform or change beliefs, values or behaviors.

2

Three branches and an eagle

The three branches of government and how they have changed · SS.5.CV.1

Open the story

Group activity: Design a class seal · How a rule becomes law

VA:Cn11.1.5

Groups design a seal for the class with two symbols and a short motto, and explain them in a gallery walk. As an extension, split the class into three branches: one group writes a classroom rule, another can approve or veto it, and a third decides if it follows the class constitution.

Discussion questions

Why did the writers of the Constitution divide power into three branches? How has the government grown since 1789?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at an old print of the arms of the United States, the same design as the Great Seal.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.5.CV.1: explain the roles and responsibilities of government officials at the local, state and national levels, and examine how the roles of the three branches of government have changed over time. The challenge lets students show SS.5.CV.1. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.5: recognize that people use art to inform or change beliefs, values or behaviors.

3

Yankee Doodle and the tea

How the government pays for the goods and services it provides · SS.5.EC.3 · SS.5.H.1

Open the story

Group activity: One song, two meanings

MU:Cn11.1.5

Sing the verse of "Yankee Doodle" twice: first as the British soldiers who used it to mock the colonists, then as the colonists who sang it with pride. Discuss how the same words can change meaning depending on who sings them and why.

Discussion questions

What public services do our taxes pay for? Why did the colonists protest taxation without representation?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Before the Revolutionary War, British soldiers sang this old song to make fun of the colonists. They thought the colonists were silly country people. Read the verse aloud.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.5.EC.3: determine the ways in which government pays for goods and services it provides; and SS.5.H.1: create and use a chronological sequence of related events to identify cause and effect, and examine how underrepresented groups were affected. The challenge lets students show SS.5.EC.3. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): MU:Cn11.1.5: relate music to the other arts, other subjects, varied contexts and daily life.

4

The photo of the last rail

Railroads, trade and natural resources · SS.5.G.3 · SS.5.EC.1 · SS.5.H.1

Open the story

Group activity: Photo detectives

VA:Cn11.1.5

Project the 1869 photograph from the Library of Congress. Groups use See, Think, Wonder. Then each student writes a caption from the point of view of a Chinese railroad worker who is not in the photo. Make clear that the caption is the student's own writing, not a historical quotation.

Discussion questions

Who benefited from the railroad, and who was harmed? Whose stories are missing from the famous photos?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at a photograph taken on May 10, 1869, when the last rail was laid.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.5.G.3: explain how human settlements and technological advancements have impacted natural resources; and SS.5.EC.1: analyze why and how individuals, businesses and nations around the world specialize and trade; and SS.5.H.1: create and use a chronological sequence of related events to identify cause and effect, and examine how underrepresented groups were affected. The challenge lets students show SS.5.G.3. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.5: recognize that people use art to inform or change beliefs, values or behaviors.

5

The road to freedom

The Underground Railroad in Illinois · SS.5.H.1

Open the story

Group activity: Call and response

MU:Cn11.1.5

Explain that call and response is a musical tradition with deep roots in African and African American music, heard in spirituals, work songs, gospel and the blues. The teacher (or a student volunteer) speaks or sings each line of "Go Down, Moses," and the class answers "Let my people go!" Treat the song with respect: it was created by enslaved people who longed for freedom.

Discussion questions

What risks did freedom seekers and their helpers take? Why were songs important to people who were enslaved?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Read these lines from the spiritual “Go Down, Moses” slowly, as if you were singing them.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.5.H.1: create and use a chronological sequence of related events to identify cause and effect, and examine how underrepresented groups were affected. The challenge lets students show SS.5.H.1. The women who marched in front

Women's right to vote and who was still left out · SS.5.CV.2 · SS.5.CV.4

Open the story

Group activity: Banners for a cause

VA:Cn11.1.5

In groups, students choose a fair change they would like at school and design a banner or poster for it, using images and a few strong words, like the suffragists. Each group gives a 30-second speech to convince the class.

Discussion questions

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the cover of the official program of the 1913 suffrage procession in Washington, D.C.

A ticket to the North

The Great Migration and the music that came to Chicago · SS.5.H.1 · SS.5.G.2

Open the story

Group activity: Listening session · Radio play

MU:Cn11.1.5

Play recordings of gospel by Mahalia Jackson or music by Thomas A. Dorsey, chosen from your library or a public broadcaster, and have students clap along with the blues rhythm from the story. Then groups write and perform a short radio play about a fictional family deciding whether to move north, clearly presented as fiction, using the facts from the story.

Discussion questions

What pushed people to leave the South, and what pulled them to Chicago? How did the Great Migration change the music of the country?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Press the button to hear a simple blues bass line. Tap your foot on the beat. Then read the lines below from a poem by Langston Hughes, written in 1925, about a blues piano player.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The challenge lets students show SS.5.H.1. When the wind took the soil

The Dust Bowl and how disasters move people · SS.5.G.4

Open the story

Group activity: Gallery walk of Dust Bowl photos

VA:Cn11.1.5

Print several Farm Security Administration photographs from the Library of Congress and post them around the room. Students walk in pairs and write on sticky notes: one detail they see, one thing it makes them feel and one question. Repeat the soil and fan experiment from the story.

How did people and nature together cause the Dust Bowl? How can photographs change what people believe or do?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at a photograph that Arthur Rothstein took in Cimarron County, Oklahoma, in 1936.

10

Rewards, fines and interest

How incentives change what people do, and what interest is · SS.5.EC.2 · SS.5.EC.4

Open the story

Group activity: Ad makers

MA:Cn11.1.5

Groups create two ads for the same product: one honest ad, and one that uses tricks ("today only," "free," bright colors). The class votes on which ad works better and then names every trick used.

Discussion questions

When do incentives work better than fines? What is interest, and who pays it?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at the bake sale poster that Athena and Elías made. Read it like a detective.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.5.EC.2: discover how positive incentives (such as sale prices and earning money) and negative consequences (such as fines) influence behavior in the U.S. economy and around the world; and SS.5.EC.4: explain that interest is the price the borrower pays for using someone else's money. The challenge lets students show SS.5.EC.2. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): MA:Cn11.1.5: examine how media artworks and ideas relate to personal, social and community life.

11

Two countries, two maps

Maps at different scales and comparing the governments of the U.S. and Canada · SS.5.G.1 · SS.5.CV.3

Open the story

Group activity: Two ways to choose a leader

VA:Cn11.1.5

Run two mock elections. First, the class votes for a "Congress" and, separately, for a "president." Then run a parliamentary version: students vote for representatives, and the leader of the group with the most seats becomes "prime minister." Compare the results and the process. Add a map scavenger hunt with maps of different scales.

Discussion questions

How are the governments of the United States and Canada alike and different? Why do maps change over time?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Look at a map of the United States made by John Melish in 1816.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). The story introduces SS.5.G.1: use print and digital maps of different scales to describe the locations of cultural and environmental characteristics; and SS.5.CV.3: compare and contrast the U.S. government to other global governments in their structure and systems of governing, and how they affect equitable outcomes. The challenge lets students show SS.5.CV.3. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): VA:Cn11.1.5: recognize that people use art to inform or change beliefs, values or behaviors.

12

The class museum

Review of the whole year · SS.5.H.3 · SS.5.CV · SS.5.G · SS.5.EC · SS.5.H

Open the story

Group activity: Class museum

TH:Cn11.2.5

Each student or group prepares an exhibit with a central claim, two pieces of evidence and one artifact (a real object, a copy or a drawing) with a museum label. Invite families to visit, like in the story.

Discussion questions

What is the central claim of your exhibit? Whose perspectives did you include?

Pause to connect (done alone in the story)

Museums use artifacts to tell stories about the past. Imagine that, 100 years from now, a museum shows one object from your life today.

Illinois Learning Standards for Social Science (revised; in effect since the 2022–2023 school year). This story reviews the Grade 5 standards in civics, geography, economics and history (SS.5.CV · SS.5.G · SS.5.EC · SS.5.H) and introduces SS.5.H.3: summarize the central claim in a work of history. Arts connection (Illinois Arts Learning Standards, Anchor Standard 11, Connecting): TH:Cn11.2.5: investigate historical and cultural artifacts of a time period to understand the people who lived then.

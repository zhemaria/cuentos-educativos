The story

Mr. Mensah placed a on the table under a straight copper wire. The needle pointed north, as always. "Watch," he said, and he connected the wire to a battery. The needle swung sideways. When he disconnected the wire, it swung back to north. "Do it again!" said Daniel.

"In 1820, a Danish scientist named Hans Christian Oersted saw exactly this," said Mr. Mensah. "He had been looking for a connection between electricity and magnetism for two years. During a lecture in Copenhagen in April 1820, he held a compass near a wire with an , and the needle moved." The needle moved so little that the audience barely noticed. But Oersted knew it was important.

"Why did the needle move?" asked Salma. "Because an electric current makes a around the wire," said Mr. Mensah. "The compass needle is a tiny magnet, so the field pushes it." "So electricity can make magnetism," said Athena. "That's the big idea. Almost every motor in the world, from your fan to an electric car, comes from it."

Then he handed out iron nails, long copper wires and batteries. "If one straight wire makes a weak magnetic field, what happens if you wrap the wire around a nail many times?" he asked. "You make an : a magnet that works only while the current flows."

Daniel wrapped his wire around the nail 10 times and connected the battery. The nail picked up 3 paper clips. When he disconnected the battery, the clips fell off. "It turns off!" he said. "A regular magnet can't do that."

"Now let's ask a testable question," said Mr. Mensah. "What makes the electromagnet stronger?" "In a fair test, you change only one at a time," he reminded them. "Keep the same nail and the same battery, and change only the number of turns in the ." With 20 turns, the nail picked up 7 clips. With 30 turns, it picked up 11.

Athena's group tested something else. They kept 20 turns but used two batteries instead of one. The nail picked up 13 clips. "More turns in the coil make it stronger," said Athena, "and more current makes it stronger, too." Salma made a table of the data and drew a graph. The line went up as the number of turns went up.

"Where do people use electromagnets?" asked Mr. Mensah. Daniel thought of the scrapyard near his uncle's shop, where a giant crane lifts old cars with a round magnet and then drops them. "They turn the magnet off to let go!" he said. "Exactly," said Mr. Mensah. "All of that started with one little needle that jumped in 1820."

Glossary

compass
A tool with a small magnetic needle that turns to point north.
brújula: Un instrumento con una pequeña aguja magnética que gira para señalar el norte.
electric current
The flow of electric charge through a material, like a wire.
corriente eléctrica: El paso de carga eléctrica a través de un material, como un cable.
magnetic field
The area around a magnet or an electric current where magnetic forces act.
campo magnético: La zona alrededor de un imán o de una corriente eléctrica donde actúan fuerzas magnéticas.
electromagnet
A magnet made by sending an electric current through a coil of wire. It can be turned on and off.
electroimán: Un imán que se forma al hacer pasar corriente eléctrica por una bobina de alambre. Se puede encender y apagar.
coil
Wire wrapped around and around in a spiral, like a spring.
bobina: Alambre enrollado muchas veces en espiral, como un resorte.
variable
Something that can change in an experiment. In a fair test, you change only one variable at a time.
variable: Algo que puede cambiar en un experimento. En una prueba justa se cambia solo una variable a la vez.

Questions

Elige la respuesta correcta. Si te equivocas, el cuento te muestra dónde está la respuesta.

  1. AQuestion A. What did Oersted see in 1820?

    ¿Qué vio Oersted en 1820?

    During a lecture in Copenhagen, he held a compass near a wire with an electric current, and the needle moved.

  2. BQuestion B. Why did the compass needle move?

    ¿Por qué se movió la aguja de la brújula?

    An electric current makes a magnetic field around the wire, and the field pushes the tiny magnet in the compass.

  3. CQuestion C. How do you make an electromagnet?

    ¿Cómo se hace un electroimán?

    You wrap a wire around a nail many times and send an electric current through it.

  4. DQuestion D. What makes a test of the number of turns a fair test?

    ¿Qué hace que la prueba del número de vueltas sea una prueba justa?

    In a fair test you change only one variable: same nail, same battery, only the number of turns changes.

  5. EQuestion E. What two things made the electromagnet stronger?

    ¿Qué dos cosas hicieron más fuerte el electroimán?

    More turns made it stronger, and two batteries (more current) made it stronger, too.

Challenge: explain your thinking

Plan an experiment

Write a testable question about electromagnets that the class did not test, for example about the size of the nail. Then plan a fair test: what will you change, what will you keep the same, and what will you measure?

Planea un experimento. Escribe una pregunta comprobable sobre los electroimanes que la clase no investigó, por ejemplo sobre el tamaño del clavo. Después planea una prueba justa: ¿qué vas a cambiar, qué vas a mantener igual y qué vas a medir?

Answer in your notebook or talk it over with a classmate. Use evidence from the story.

Responde en tu cuaderno o coméntalo con un compañero. Usa evidencia del cuento.

Check your thinking

Example question: Does a thicker nail make a stronger electromagnet? Change: the thickness of the nail. Keep the same: the number of turns (for example, 20), the same wire, the same battery and the same paper clips. Measure: how many paper clips the nail picks up. Repeat each try three times to be sure of the results.

The story

One Saturday in October, Great-Grandpa took Israel and Lucía to Michigan Avenue. They stopped in front of a stone tower that looked like a castle. "This is the Water Tower," he said. "It's one of the few buildings that survived the Great Chicago Fire."

"The started on the night of October 8, 1871, in a small barn behind the O'Leary family's house on DeKoven Street." No one knows for sure how it began.

"Why did it get so big?" asked Lucía. "For several reasons," Great-Grandpa explained. "That summer it had hardly rained: there was a , and everything was dry. A very strong wind was blowing, and almost the whole city was made of : the houses, the roofs and even the sidewalks."

The wind carried sparks from one roof to the next. The fire began to north and east. In the early morning of October 9, the flames jumped the Chicago River, burned downtown and reached the waterworks. Without water, the firefighters could hardly put it out.

Thousands of families fled toward Lake Michigan with whatever they could carry. Some stood in the water to escape the heat. At last, on October 10, rain began to fall, and the fire went out.

"It was a tragedy," Great-Grandpa said softly. "About 300 people died and about 17,000 buildings burned. Nearly 100,000 people were left ." That was almost one out of every three people in the city.

"But Chicago didn't give up," he went on. "Help came from many cities in the country and around the world, and people began to the city. This time, downtown buildings had to be made of , stone and iron, which don't burn as easily."

A few years later, some of the first skyscrapers in the world were built in Chicago. And today, one of the four red stars on the city flag stands for the Great Fire.

Israel and Lucía made a timeline: October 8, the fire starts; October 9, it crosses the river and burns downtown; October 10, the rain comes; afterward, the rebuilding. "The city learned to be stronger from something bad," said Israel.

Glossary

fire
A big, out-of-control fire that burns things that shouldn't burn, like houses or forests.
drought
A long time with very little rain, when the land and plants dry out.
wood
The material that comes from tree trunks. It burns easily.
spread
To move from one place to another, like fire passing from one house to the next.
homeless
Without a home, for example after losing it in a disaster.
rebuild
To build again what was destroyed.
brick
A block of baked clay used to build walls. It doesn't burn like wood.

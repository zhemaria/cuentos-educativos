The story

At the end of the year, the school library became a museum. Each of the seven friends set up an , and families walked from table to table. Mr. Okafor gave each friend one rule: "Your exhibit must have a , the main idea you want to prove, and evidence to support it."

Athena's table had her drawing of the 1856 painting of the Constitutional Convention. Her central claim was: "A source made long after an event shows the artist's ideas, so we must ask who made it, when and why." Next to it, she added her own drawing with the people who had been left out.

Salma's table had Elías's old drawing of a tree with three branches, a picture of the Great Seal and her store receipt. "The government has three branches so that no one becomes too powerful," she explained, "and it pays for public services with taxes. That's why the colonists protested at the Boston Tea Party: they had no say in their taxes."

Sophia stood beside a model steam train. "The railroad helped the country trade and grow," she said, "but it hurt the bison, the Plains nations and the Chinese workers who built it and were left out of the photos." A grandfather nodded slowly. "I never thought about it that way."

Israel had a map of the Underground Railroad in Illinois. Selena had a banner she had painted for women's suffrage, with Ida B. Wells marching beside the Illinois women. "People won the right to vote little by little," Selena said, "and each change happened because people kept pushing."

Daniel's was the most personal: a copy of his great-grandmother's train ticket and a speaker playing gospel music. "The Great Migration changed where millions of people lived, and it changed the music of the whole country," he said. His great-grandmother was standing right there, smiling.

The last table had Elías's tray of grass and soil from the Dust Bowl experiment and the bake sale poster. "Disasters can make people move," Elías said, "and incentives can make people change what they do." Everyone who walked by got a free cookie. "That's an incentive to visit my exhibit," Elías admitted.

At the end of the night, Mr. Okafor gathered the class around a long on the wall, from 1773 to 1968. "Every exhibit tells a different part of the story," he said, "and every story has more than one . That's what historians do: they make a claim, they look for evidence, and they listen to the people who were left out."

Daniel's great-grandmother tapped Mr. Okafor on the shoulder. "You know," she said, "this is the first museum where I'm one of the ." Everyone laughed, and she laughed loudest of all.

Pause to connect

🎭 Theater · TH:Cn11.2.5

Museums use artifacts to tell stories about the past. Imagine that, 100 years from now, a museum shows one object from your life today.

Los museos usan artefactos para contar historias del pasado. Imagina que, dentro de 100 años, un museo muestra un objeto de tu vida de hoy.

How to do it: Choose an object from your home: a toy, a photo, a ticket, a tool or anything else. Look at it as if you were a historian from the future.

Cómo hacerlo: Elige un objeto de tu casa: un juguete, una foto, un boleto, una herramienta o cualquier otra cosa. Míralo como si fueras un historiador del futuro.

  1. Write a museum label for your object: what it is, who made it, when it was used and what it was for.Escribe una etiqueta de museo para tu objeto: qué es, quién lo hizo, cuándo se usaba y para qué servía.
  2. What will people in the future learn about life in our time from your object? What will it NOT tell them?¿Qué aprenderá la gente del futuro sobre nuestra época con tu objeto? ¿Qué NO les dirá?

Think about it, then answer in your notebook or talk it over with someone at home.

Check your thinking

Example: "Bus card, Chicago, 2026. Made by the city's transit system. Children and adults used it to ride the trains and buses." Historians in the future would learn that people in Chicago used public transportation, but not who rode it, where they went or how they felt. Every artifact tells part of a story, never all of it.

Glossary

central claim
The main idea that a writer or historian wants to prove.
idea central: La idea principal que un escritor o un historiador quiere demostrar.
evidence
Facts, sources or examples that support a claim.
evidencia: Datos, fuentes o ejemplos que apoyan una idea.
artifact
An object made or used by people in the past.
artefacto: Un objeto hecho o usado por las personas en el pasado.
exhibit
A display in a museum that teaches about a topic.
exhibición: Una muestra en un museo que enseña sobre un tema.
timeline
A line that shows events in the order in which they happened.
línea del tiempo: Una línea que muestra los sucesos en el orden en que ocurrieron.
perspective
A point of view: the way a person or group sees an event.
perspectiva: Un punto de vista: la forma en que una persona o un grupo ve un suceso.

Example: Claim: "The Great Migration changed Chicago." Evidence: Chicago's Black population grew from about 44,000 in 1910 to more than 230,000 in 1930; and Thomas A. Dorsey and Mahalia Jackson helped make gospel music famous from Chicago.

Summarize a history paragraph

Read this short history paragraph. In one sentence, write its central claim in your own words. Then copy one detail the author uses to support it.

Between about 1916 and 1970, around six million African Americans left the rural South and moved to cities in the North and the West. Many were escaping unfair laws, violence and low pay, and many hoped to find factory jobs. In Chicago, the newcomers built churches, newspapers and businesses, and musicians brought the blues and gospel with them. They also faced crowded housing and unfair rules that kept many families out of some neighborhoods. The Great Migration was one of the largest movements of people in the history of the United States, and it changed Chicago forever.

Resume un párrafo de historia. Lee este breve párrafo de historia. En una oración, escribe con tus propias palabras su afirmación central. Después copia un detalle que el autor usa para apoyarla.

Check your thinking

The central claim is the big idea the whole paragraph supports, often near the beginning or the end. Here it is something like: "The Great Migration was a huge movement of African Americans that changed Chicago." Supporting details include the six million people who moved, the churches, newspapers and businesses they built, and the blues and gospel music they brought. A detail that only tells one fact, like the years, is evidence, not the claim.

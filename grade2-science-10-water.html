The story

It was January in Chicago. Grandpa took Lucía and Israel to see Lake Michigan. Everything was white. There were mountains of ice along the shore, and the sand was covered with snow.

"Did the lake freeze?" Israel asked. "Only near the shore," Grandpa answered. "There, the water is : it is ice. Farther out, the water is still and moves with the waves."

Grandpa explained the difference. A solid has its own shape, like an ice cube. A liquid does not have its own shape: it takes the shape of its container, like water in a glass.

Back home, Grandpa took out his , a round model of the Earth. "Why is almost everything blue?" Lucía asked. "Because most of the Earth is covered with water," said Grandpa. "And almost all of that water is in the oceans."

"Ocean water is ," Grandpa went on. "We cannot drink it." The water in rivers and in most lakes, like Lake Michigan, is . It has very little salt.

Then Grandpa pointed to the white parts at the top and bottom of the globe. "Here, at the poles, it is so cold that the water is always frozen. There is also solid water high on some mountains."

Huge masses of ice that move very slowly down mountains are called . "They are like the ice on the lake, but giant!" said Israel.

That night, Lucía drew a map of Earth's water: oceans, rivers, lakes, snow, and glaciers. Next to it she wrote: "Water can be liquid or solid, and it is found in many places."

Glossary

solid
A state in which something has its own shape, like ice or a rock.
liquid
A state in which something has no shape of its own and takes the shape of its container, like water.
globe
A round model of the Earth that shows where land and water are.
salt water
The water in oceans and seas. It has a lot of salt, and we cannot drink it.
fresh water
The water in rivers and most lakes. It has very little salt.
glacier
A huge mass of ice that forms on mountains or near the poles and moves very slowly.
